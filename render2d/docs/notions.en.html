1. The rendering pipeline

The renderer is a command, in the spirit of ogr2ogr: data and a style in, a map out. In between, six steps, and only the last one depends on the output format.

  1. Reading — GDAL/OGR reads the data, whatever their format.
  2. Preparation — reprojection, clipping to the extent, simplification.
  3. Style — for each feature, choose the symbolizers to apply (the SLD model, written in YSLD).
  4. Ground → pixels — each vertex goes through a matrix.
  5. Drawing — we talk to an abstract "canvas".
  6. Surface — Cairo writes the result as PNG, SVG or PDF.

The first chapters cover steps 4, 5 and 6: the core, without which nothing is displayed. GDAL reading and styling come next.

2. Two frames: the ground and the image

The ground frame is the data's. In Belgian Lambert 72 (EPSG:31370), coordinates are metres, X grows towards the east, Y grows towards the north. A point in the centre of Brussels is about X = 149,000, Y = 170,000.

The image frame is the pixels'. The origin (0, 0) is the top-left corner, x grows to the right, y grows downwards. It is the convention of almost every image format and every drawing library, Cairo included.

The only real difficulty is there: the two vertical axes go in opposite directions. Going up towards the north means going down in the image's row numbers.

3. From the ground to pixels, by hand

We want a 1000 × 1000 pixel image showing the square of ground going from 150,000 to 155,000 in X and from 160,000 to 165,000 in Y. This square is 5000 m on a side.

Step A — how many metres per pixel?
  1. Ground width: 155,000 − 150,000 = 5000 m.
  2. Image width: 1000 pixels.
  3. Resolution: 5000 ÷ 1000 = 5 m per pixel.
We call this resolution res. Its inverse, 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2 pixel per metre, is the transformation's scale; we call it s.
Step B — a point's column (x)

Take the point X = 152,500, Y = 162,500.

  1. Distance from the ground's left edge: 152,500 − 150,000 = 2500 m.
  2. In pixels: 2500 ÷ 5 = 500.
Step C — the same point's row (y)

Here we measure from the ground's top edge, that is from Ymax = 165,000, because row 0 of the image is at the top.

  1. Distance from the top edge: 165,000 − 162,500 = 2500 m.
  2. In pixels: 2500 ÷ 5 = 500.

The point lands at (500, 500), in the centre of the image, as expected.

In short, with Xmin = left edge and Ymax = top edge:

x = (X − Xmin) ÷ res
y = (Ymax − Y) ÷ res

Written with a zoom factor zoom = 1 ÷ res, it is the familiar x·zoom + offset.x and offset.y − y·zoom of many map engines.

4. When the extent and the image do not have the same shape

Asking for 1000 × 1000 pixels for a square piece of ground is the easy case. But what if we ask for the same ground on 1000 × 600 pixels?

  1. Horizontally: 5000 m ÷ 1000 px = 5 m/px.
  2. Vertically: 5000 m ÷ 600 px ≈ 8.33 m/px.

If we kept both, a pixel would represent 5 m in width and 8.33 m in height: the map would be squashed, circles would become ovals. A map must keep square pixels.

The rule: take the larger of the two resolutions, here 8.33 m/px, so that all the requested ground fits in the image, and centre it. The ground actually shown gets wider:

  1. Width shown: 1000 px × 8.33 m/px ≈ 8333 m, instead of 5000 m.
  2. Surplus: 8333 − 5000 = 3333 m, i.e. ≈ 1667 m on each side.
  3. Centre of the requested ground: X = 152,500. New left edge: 152,500 − 8333 ÷ 2 ≈ 148,333.

This is not an error, it is a decision: we show at least what was asked for, without ever distorting it. The renderer says in its warnings that the extent was widened. (For tiles, the question does not arise: the tile and its extent are both square.)

5. The same thing as a matrix

Let us expand the two formulas with s = 1 ÷ res = 0.2:

x = s·X − s·Xmin   = 0.2·X − 30,000
y = −s·Y + s·Ymax  = −0.2·Y + 33,000

(0.2 × 150,000 = 30,000 and 0.2 × 165,000 = 33,000.)

These are two formulas of the same form: some X, some Y, plus a constant. This is called an affine transformation. It is written with six numbers a, b, c, d, e, f:

x = a·X + c·Y + e
y = b·X + d·Y + f

Here: a = 0.2; b = 0; c = 0; d = −0.2; e = −30,000; f = 33,000.

6. The way back: from pixels to the ground

We will often need the inverse: which ground coordinates correspond to pixel (0, 0)? To a mouse click? To the four corners of a tile? In our simple case, it is enough to "undo" the formulas:

X = Xmin + x·res = 150,000 + 5·x
Y = Ymax − y·res = 165,000 − 5·y
  1. Pixel (0, 0), top-left corner: X = 150,000, Y = 165,000.
  2. Pixel (1000, 1000), bottom-right corner: X = 155,000, Y = 160,000.
  3. Pixel (0.5; 0.5), the centre of the first pixel: X = 150,002.5, Y = 164,997.5 (more on this in chapter 9).

The general formula

For any affine matrix, we go through the determinant of the 2 × 2 block:

det = a·d − b·c
X = ( d·(x − e) − c·(y − f)) ÷ det
Y = (−b·(x − e) + a·(y − f)) ÷ det
With our numbers, for pixel (500, 500)
  1. det = 0.2 × (−0.2) − 0 × 0 = −0.04.
  2. x − e = 500 − (−30,000) = 30,500; y − f = 500 − 33,000 = −32,500.
  3. X = (−0.2 × 30,500 − 0) ÷ (−0.04) = −6100 ÷ (−0.04) = 152,500.
  4. Y = (0 + 0.2 × (−32,500)) ÷ (−0.04) = −6500 ÷ (−0.04) = 162,500.

The determinant tells something concrete, as in 3D:

  • Its absolute value is a ratio of areas. 0.04 means that one square metre of ground becomes 0.04 square pixel (0.2 px × 0.2 px). A 5 m × 5 m = 25 m² square becomes 25 × 0.04 = 1 pixel.
  • Its negative sign signals a mirror: the flip of the Y axis. It matters in chapter 11, because a mirror reverses the winding direction of outlines.
  • If it were 0, the transformation would squash everything onto a line and could not be inverted. The code must check it (a zero-width extent, for instance).

7. The map scale

SLD style rules have scale ranges ("visible only below 1:5000"). So we need to know which scale an image represents. The OGC sets a convention: a "standard" pixel measures 0.28 mm on screen.

  1. A pixel represents 5 m of ground (our resolution).
  2. That pixel measures 0.28 mm = 0.00028 m on screen.
  3. Scale: 5 ÷ 0.00028 ≈ 17,857, i.e. 1:17,857.

A rule "visible below 1:5000" therefore does not apply here. It would need a resolution of 5000 × 0.00028 = 1.4 m/px or less.

The scale follows directly from the matrix: res = 1 ÷ |a| (if the map is not rotated), then scale = res ÷ 0.00028.

8. Precision: why double and not float

A float stores a number with 24 significant binary digits. The larger the number, the larger the gap between two representable values. This gap is called the ulp (unit in the last place).

Computing the ulp
  1. Find the power of 2 just below the number: for 150,000, it is 217 = 131,072 (since 218 = 262,144 is too large).
  2. The ulp is 217 − 23 = 2−6 = 1 ÷ 64 ≈ 1.6 cm.
  3. In Web Mercator, Y ≈ 6,500,000 for Belgium: the power of 2 just below is 222 = 4,194,304, so the ulp is 222 − 23 = 50 cm.

Half a metre of imprecision is invisible at the scale of a country, but huge when zoomed on a parcel: at 0.1 m/px, vertices would jump by 5 pixels. A double has 53 binary digits: at 6,500,000, its ulp is about 1 nanometre. Project rule: everything in double until the pixels are computed.

9. The half pixel

For Cairo (as for PostScript, SVG, AGG), whole coordinates fall on the edges of pixels, not at their centre. Pixel no. 4 occupies the interval from x = 4 to x = 5; its centre is at 4.5.

Consequence: a 1-pixel-wide vertical line placed at x = 4 covers the interval from 3.5 to 4.5. It covers half of pixel 3 and half of pixel 4. Anti-aliasing then paints two columns at 50 %: a blurred grey 2-pixel line instead of a sharp black one.

Placed at x = 4.5, the same line covers exactly the interval from 4 to 5: one column, 100 % black.

For cartography, we do not force everything onto centres: parcel outlines fall where they fall, and anti-aliasing is made precisely for that. But "technical" elements (the map frame, a grid, thin horizontal and vertical lines) gain from being aligned. It is also a key notion for any software rasterizer: it is the whole question of computing a pixel's coverage.

10. The canvas: a draughtsman's vocabulary

The renderer never talks to Cairo directly. It talks to a canvas: a small interface holding the basic vocabulary of vector drawing. This vocabulary has hardly changed since PostScript (Adobe, 1984); Cairo, SVG, PDF, AGG, Blend2D and the browsers' canvas all speak it.

The same path can be filled then stroked (Cairo's cairo_fill_preserve). Our canvas allows it with PathMode::Keep, and the fillAndStroke shortcut does both at once.

In the code, these operations follow the project's style (camelCase): moveTo, lineTo, curveTo, closePath, fill, stroke, save, restore, plus clear to paint the background.

The stroke state

A stroke has a few settings, all present in SLD:

  • the width, in pixels (see chapter 12);
  • the ends (cap): cut flat, rounded, or square, sticking out by half a width;
  • the joins between segments: pointed (miter), rounded, or bevelled;
  • dashes: a list of lengths "dash, gap, dash, gap…".

And a colour, with its alpha (opacity), which lets an orthophoto show through a 40 % fill, for instance.

11. Filling a polygon with holes

A city block with an inner courtyard is a polygon with two rings: the exterior and a hole. Both go into the same path, then fill is called. But how does the engine know that the inside of the small ring must stay empty? There are two rules.

The even-odd rule

From a point, draw a half-line to infinity and count how many outlines it crosses. An odd number: the point is inside, paint it. An even number: outside, do not paint.

  • A point in the built part crosses 1 outline (the exterior): odd, paint.
  • A point in the courtyard crosses 2 outlines (the hole, then the exterior): even, do not paint.

The nonzero winding rule

Count while taking the direction of the outlines into account: +1 when an outline crosses the half-line one way, −1 the other way. If the total is not 0, paint.

  • If the hole runs in the opposite direction to the exterior, the courtyard gets +1 − 1 = 0: it stays empty.
  • If the hole runs in the same direction, the courtyard gets +1 + 1 = 2: it is painted, the hole disappears.

Yet the direction of rings varies between formats: the shapefile puts the exterior clockwise, GeoJSON (RFC 7946) anticlockwise, and many real files respect neither. Not to mention that our matrix, with its negative determinant, reverses every winding direction.

Project rule: polygons are filled with even-odd. It gives the right result whatever the direction of the rings. (It has one limit: two distinct polygons overlapping in the same path would leave a "hole" where they intersect. So grouping several features into a single path must be done with care.)

12. Why we transform the vertices ourselves

Cairo can apply a matrix on its own (cairo_set_matrix). We could therefore give it the ground coordinates and the matrix, and let it work. We do not, for three reasons.

  1. Line widths. Cairo interprets the width in the frame in force at the time of the stroke. With our 0.2 px/m matrix, a width of "1" would mean 1 metre, i.e. 0.2 pixel: an almost invisible line, which would moreover get thicker when zooming. In cartography, we want a 1-pixel line at every scale.
  2. Simplification. Once the vertices are in pixels, those less than a pixel away from the previous one can be removed. A border of 50,000 vertices drawn on 1000 pixels of width needs only a few thousand points; the drawing work is divided accordingly.
  3. Understanding and control. The matrix is ours, tested by us, identical for Cairo and for any future engine. Cairo only sees pixels, with its matrix left at identity.

13. One surface, three outputs: PNG, SVG, PDF

Cairo draws on a surface. The drawing calls are the same; only the creation of the surface changes:

For vector outputs, the image unit becomes the typographic point. Our matrix does not change its form: it simply converts metres into points instead of metres into pixels. Example: a map the width of an A4 page, 210 mm = 210 ÷ 25.4 × 72 ≈ 595 points.

For PNG, we choose the ARGB32 format: 4 bytes per pixel (alpha, red, green, blue), which allows a transparent background, essential for tiles layered in Leaflet. A 1000 × 1000 image therefore takes 1000 × 1000 × 4 = 4,000,000 bytes in memory, about 4 MB.

15. Simple Features: the OGC model step 2

All the sources GDAL can read (shapefile, GeoPackage, PostGIS, GeoJSON…) present their geometries to it in one model: the OGC's Simple Features, also standardised as ISO 19125. This model defines the geometry types and the WKT (text) and WKB (binary) encodings.

TypeWhat it isReal example
Pointa positiona bollard, a tree
LineStringa series of points joined by segmentsa street's axis
Polygonan exterior ring and 0, 1 or several interior rings (holes)a block with a courtyard
MultiPoint, MultiLineString, MultiPolygonseveral geometries of the same type, forming one featurea municipality with an island
GeometryCollectiona mix of typesrare in cartography

The standard sets rules that suit us:

  • a ring is closed: its last point repeats the first;
  • holes are inside the exterior ring;
  • the polygons of a MultiPolygon do not overlap.

That last rule has a direct consequence for chapter 11: all the rings of a MultiPolygon can go into a single path and be filled with even-odd safely. The only danger, two overlapping polygons in the same path, is excluded by the standard.

But real data do not always respect the standard: badly closed rings, self-intersections, holes spilling out. True to the project's principle, we draw anyway, and report it.

16. Our flat geometry

GDAL represents a geometry as a hierarchy of objects (a polygon contains rings, which contain points). For drawing, we convert it into a structure of our own, made of two flat arrays:

struct Part { std::size_t start; std::size_t count; };   // a ring or a line

struct Geometry {
    GeomType           type;     // Point, Line or Polygon
    std::vector<Point> points;   // all the vertices, one after the other
    std::vector<Part>  parts;    // where each ring starts, and how many points it has
};
The demo's block with a courtyard, flattened
  1. The exterior ring has 5 points (4 corners, plus the first repeated to close it): they occupy slots 0 to 4 of points.
  2. The courtyard also has 5 points: slots 5 to 9.
  3. parts therefore holds two entries: {start = 0, count = 5} and {start = 5, count = 5}.

A municipality with an island (a MultiPolygon of two polygons without holes) gives exactly the same shape: two parts. A MultiPolygon one of whose polygons has a courtyard gives three parts. For even-odd drawing, there is no need to know which hole belongs to which polygon.

Why not use GDAL's objects directly?

  • To keep GDAL in a single folder, like Cairo: backend/gdal/ converts, and the rest of the renderer only knows Geometry. The day the data are read differently (for instance in columns, with GDAL's Arrow interface), only that folder changes.
  • Because contiguous arrays are fast to walk through, and easy to transform in one go (reprojection, matrix, simplification).
  • Because the "parts" matter: a single list of coordinates without them loses the holes and the islands.

One feature is converted, drawn, then the next: we stay in the pipeline, without ever keeping the whole layer in memory.

17. The GDAL chain

GDAL organises a vector source in four levels:

GDALDataset      the source: a file, a folder, a database
  └─ OGRLayer    a layer: "parcels", "buildings"
       └─ OGRFeature      a feature: attributes and a geometry
            └─ OGRGeometry   OGRPolygon, OGRMultiPolygon, OGRLineString…

A shapefile is a source with a single layer. A GeoPackage or a PostGIS database holds several. For our code, it is the same: open a source, pick a layer, walk through its features.

Reading looks like this (simplified form, from GDAL's official tutorial):

GDALAllRegister();                                   // once at start-up
GDALDatasetUniquePtr ds(GDALDataset::Open("parcels.shp", GDAL_OF_VECTOR));
OGRLayer* layer = ds->GetLayer(0);
layer->SetSpatialFilterRect(xmin, ymin, xmax, ymax); // only the view (chapter 18)

for (auto& feature : layer) {                        // one feature at a time
    const OGRGeometry* g = feature->GetGeometryRef();
    // g → flat Geometry → drawing
}

Three points of attention:

  • Object ownership. GDALDatasetUniquePtr and the features of the for loop are "unique" pointers (RAII): they free the memory on their own when they go out of scope. The geometry belongs to its feature: we read it, we do not free it.
  • "Flattened" types. A geometry can have a Z (elevation) or an M (measure). wkbFlatten(g->getGeometryType()) gives the 2D type; for drawing, Z and M are ignored.
  • The coordinate system. GDAL returns coordinates as they are in the source. Until reprojection exists, the source and the map are assumed to share a system. If the source declares something else, we draw anyway and warn.

18. The spatial filter

A municipality can have 30,000 parcels. If the view shows only a thirtieth of them, reading the 30,000 to draw 1,000 would be a waste. The spatial filter asks GDAL to return only the features touching a rectangle.

Each format has its index to answer quickly, and GDAL uses it for us:

  • shapefile: a .qix file (created once with ogrinfo parcels.shp -sql "CREATE SPATIAL INDEX ON parcels"); without it, GDAL walks through the whole file but returns only the useful features;
  • GeoPackage: an SQLite R-tree;
  • PostGIS: a GiST index, queried on the server.

Why ask for a little more than the view

The filter compares geometries, but we draw thick lines. A line whose axis passes just outside the image can still bite into it with half its width.

The margin, with numbers
  1. Thickest line of the style: 5 px. It sticks out from its axis by 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5 px on each side.
  2. At 0.5 m per pixel (the demo scene), 2.5 px are 2.5 × 0.5 = 1.25 m.
  3. At 5 m per pixel (the example of the first chapters), the same 2.5 px are 2.5 × 5 = 12.5 m.

So the filter's rectangle is widened by this margin on each side. For tiles, the margin matters even more, with labels and symbols overflowing from one tile to the next.

GDAL's filter is a first sort: depending on the format, it may return a few features that do not really touch the view (their bounding rectangle touches it, not their shape). That does not matter: Cairo only draws what falls in the image anyway.

At step 2, the style is fixed, but the shape of the code is already that of the future SLD symbolizers: one function per geometry type, receiving the geometry, the matrix and a style.

An important remark: a point's circle radius is in pixels, like line widths (chapter 12). A bollard stays a 3-pixel dot, whatever the scale.

20. A circle with four Béziers

Our canvas has no "circle" operation: it only has segments and cubic Bézier curves. That is deliberate, since it is the minimal vocabulary of every engine. A circle is therefore drawn with four quarter circles, each approximated by a Bézier.

For the quarter circle going from (r, 0) to (0, r), around the origin, the Bézier's four points are:

start      P0 = (r, 0)
control 1  P1 = (r, k·r)
control 2  P2 = (k·r, r)
end        P3 = (0, r)

The whole question is choosing k. It is chosen so that the middle of the curve falls exactly on the circle.

Computing k, step by step
  1. The middle of a cubic Bézier (t = ½) is (P0 + 3·P1 + 3·P2 + P3) ÷ 8.
  2. Its x coordinate is therefore (r + 3r + 3kr + 0) ÷ 8 = r·(4 + 3k) ÷ 8. By symmetry, y has the same value.
  3. The middle of the true quarter circle is at 45°, at x = y = r·cos 45° = r ÷ √2 ≈ 0.70711·r.
  4. Set them equal: (4 + 3k) ÷ 8 = 1 ÷ √2, so 4 + 3k = 8 ÷ √2 = 4√2 ≈ 5.65685.
  5. 3k = 5.65685 − 4 = 1.65685, so k ≈ 0.55228. It is the formula k = 4/3 × (√2 − 1).
Check with r = 10 pixels
  1. P0 = (10; 0), P1 = (10; 5.5228), P2 = (5.5228; 10), P3 = (0; 10).
  2. x of the middle = (10 + 3 × 10 + 3 × 5.5228 + 0) ÷ 8 = (10 + 30 + 16.568) ÷ 8 = 56.568 ÷ 8 = 7.0711.
  3. Distance to the centre: 7.0711 × √2 = 10.000. The middle is indeed on the circle.

Elsewhere, the curve moves very slightly away from the true circle: at most 0.027 % of the radius, i.e. 0.003 pixel for a 10-pixel dot. Invisible.

Cairo and PostScript do exactly the same internally for their arcs. And it is the kind of curve a font rasterizer knows well: letter outlines are made of these same Béziers.

22. The SLD model in four levels step 3a

The first rendering of the whole Brussels Region showed it: at 16.5 m per pixel, a 6 m façade is 0.36 pixel, narrower than its 0.8-pixel outline. The map becomes a grey mass of outlines. One style cannot suit every scale: rules are needed. That is the purpose of the OGC's SLD model (Styled Layer Descriptor), and of its Symbology Encoding part.

Style                        a layer's style
  └─ FeatureTypeStyle        one drawing pass over the whole layer
       └─ Rule               when to draw: filter (3b), scale range
            └─ Symbolizer    how to draw: polygon, line, point (text later)
  • A style contains one or more feature-styles.
  • A feature-style is a pass: all the features of the layer are walked through. Two feature-styles are two passes, the second on top of the first (chapter 25).
  • A rule says when it applies: at which scales and, at step 3b, to which features (a filter on the attributes).
  • A symbolizer says how to draw. These are our functions of chapter 19, which finally get their parameters from a file instead of constants.

23. Our YSLD

The SLD model is written in YSLD, its YAML form defined by GeoServer. Here is the buildings style that fixes the regional map: outlines up close, a flat fill without outline from afar.

name: batiments
feature-styles:
- rules:
  - name: proche
    scale: [min, 15000]           # scales larger than 1:15,000
    symbolizers:
    - polygon:
        fill-color: '#C98B6B'
        stroke-color: '#7A4A35'
        stroke-width: 0.8
  - name: loin
    scale: [15000, max]           # from 1:15,000 towards small scales
    symbolizers:
    - polygon:
        fill-color: '#8E7B6B'     # no stroke-color: no outline

The subset of step 3a

SymbolizerKeys understood
polygonfill-color, fill-opacity, and all the stroke-… keys below for the outline
linestroke-color, stroke-width, stroke-opacity, stroke-linecap (butt, round, square), stroke-linejoin (miter, round, bevel), stroke-dasharray (for instance '8 4')
pointsize (diameter in pixels) and symbols: [{mark: {shape: circle, fill-color, stroke-color, stroke-width}}]

All lengths (widths, dashes, sizes) are in pixels, SLD's default unit, consistent with chapter 12.

The default values:

  • polygon: without fill-color, no fill; without stroke-color, no outline (and a warning if other stroke-… settings are given without a colour);
  • line: a black 1-pixel line, SLD's default: a line exists to be drawn;
  • point: a 6-pixel dot, filled grey with a black outline, like SLD's "marks".

As in SLD, a line symbolizer applied to a polygon layer strokes their outline: that is how the parcels style draws boundaries only at large scales.

Two YAML traps

  • Colours must be quoted. In YAML, # starts a comment: fill-color: #C98B6B reads as an empty key followed by a comment. You must write fill-color: '#C98B6B'.
  • Indentation matters. As in Python, spaces say what belongs to what, and tabs are forbidden.

Always a result

An unknown key (a typo such as stroke-widht) stops nothing: it is ignored, and a warning gives its name and line. An unreadable value (stroke-width: thick) keeps the default value, with a warning. Only a malformed YAML file prevents reading the style; the layer is then drawn with the default style, again with a warning.

24. Scale ranges

A rule with scale: [A, B] applies when the map's scale denominator is at least A and strictly less than B. min and max mean "no limit". The denominator is computed as in chapter 7: resolution in metres per pixel, divided by 0.00028.

With the buildings style, three maps
  1. The whole Region: 16.529 m/px ÷ 0.00028 ≈ 59,033. 15,000 ≤ 59,033: the far rule, a flat fill without outline.
  2. A 2 km neighbourhood on 1000 pixels: 2 m/px ÷ 0.00028 ≈ 7,143. 7,143 < 15,000: the near rule, with outlines.
  3. The exact limit: 15,000 × 0.00028 = 4.2 m/px. At exactly 4.2 m/px, the denominator is 15,000, which belongs to [15000, max]: the far rule. The two ranges touch without overlapping; no scale is drawn twice or forgotten.

Beware of the vocabulary, a classic source of confusion: a "large scale" (1:1,000) shows little ground with much detail, a "small scale" (1:100,000) shows a lot with little detail. The denominator is small for a large scale.

25. Drawing order

What is drawn last is on top. The full order is:

  1. the layers, in the order of the command line (parcels, then buildings);
  2. within a layer, the feature-styles, in the order of the file: each is a complete pass over the features;
  3. within a pass, for each feature, all the rules that apply, in the order of the file (not "the first that matches": two rules can draw the same feature);
  4. within a rule, the symbolizers in order.

Why separate passes: double-line roads

A road is often drawn as a thick grey line (the "casing"), then a thinner white line on top (the "carriageway"):

name: routes
feature-styles:
- rules:
  - symbolizers:
    - line: {stroke-color: '#555555', stroke-width: 7, stroke-linecap: round}
- rules:
  - symbolizers:
    - line: {stroke-color: '#FFFFFF', stroke-width: 5, stroke-linecap: round}

With two feature-styles, all the casings are drawn, then all the carriageways: at a crossroads, the carriageways join cleanly in white. If both lines were in the same pass, road no. 2 would draw its grey casing over the white carriageway of road no. 1, already drawn, and every crossroads would be barred by a grey line.

The cost of a pass

A pass reads the layer again. Features are not kept in memory between passes: we stay in the pipeline, and measurements showed that reading is fast compared with drawing. A layer with two feature-styles is therefore read twice. If a measurement ever shows that this is a problem, the geometries could be kept from one pass to the next, but not before.

Three types are enough

GDAL knows about a dozen types (integer, 64-bit integer, real, text, date, time, binary, lists…). For a style filter, they come down to three: integer, real and text. A date compares as the text '2026-10-03', which works because the alphabetical order of that format is also the chronological order.

A fourth "value": NULL

A field can have no value. It is neither zero nor an empty text: it is NULL. In the Evere addresses, a door has no box number and no parent. ogrinfo shows it like this:

POLICENUM (String) = 68
BOXNUMBER (String) = (null)

Everything that follows must say what happens when a NULL is compared (chapter 28).

The traps of real data

  • Numbers written as text. In the addresses file, almost everything is text, including ZIPCODE ('1140') and POLICENUM, the house number. It is not carelessness: 291 Evere addresses have a number such as '75B', which is not a number.
  • The same name, two contents. MUNNISCODE is '21006' in the parcels, but 'https://databrussels.be/id/municipality/21006' in the addresses. A style written for one will not work on the other.
  • DBF, a format from 1983. A shapefile's attributes live in the .dbf, the format of the dBase II database. Field names are limited to 10 characters (hence STRNAMEFRE for street name, French) and each text always takes its full reserved width, padded with spaces. It matters for speed (chapter 30).

28. A rule's filter

In chapter 22, a rule said when it applies, but only by scale. It now gets a filter: a condition on each feature's attributes. In YSLD, the filter is written between ${ and }, in the OGC's CQL syntax (Common Query Language), GeoServer's:

name: adresses
feature-styles:
- rules:
  - name: portes
    filter: ${PARENTID IS NULL}         # a door, not a box
    symbolizers:
    - point:
        size: 5
        symbols:
        - mark: {shape: circle, fill-color: '#B5541C', stroke-color: '#FFFFFF', stroke-width: 1}

The subset understood

comparisonAREA < 30=, <>, <, <=, >, >=
intervalAREA BETWEEN 30 AND 10030 ≤ AREA ≤ 100, bounds included
listMUNNISCODE IN ('21006', '21015')the value is one of the list

Texts are written between single quotes, and a quote inside a text is doubled: STRNAMEFRE = 'Clos de l''Oasis'. Field names are written as they are. To avoid YAML surprises, the whole filter can be put between double quotes: filter: "${AREA < 30}".

Comparing a text and a number

What does POLICENUM < 100 mean, when POLICENUM is a text? Two choices are possible, and they do not give the same result:

  • Compare as text, letter by letter as in a dictionary: '75B' < '8' is true (7 comes before 8), and so is '106' < '68' (1 comes before 6). A classic trap.
  • Compare as numbers: '106' becomes 106, '68' becomes 68. That is what is expected. But '75B' is not a number.

Our rule: the literal decides. Written without quotes (100), it asks for a numeric comparison: the field's value is converted, and if it cannot be ('75B'), the result is unknown, as for a NULL. Written with quotes ('100'), it asks for a text comparison. The report counts the values that could not be converted, so that such a case does not go unnoticed.

True, false… and unknown

What is AREA < 30 for a building whose area is NULL? Neither true nor false: we do not know. We follow SQL's three-valued logic, and the rule applies only if the filter is true:

A trueA falseA unknown
A AND unknownunknownfalseunknown
A OR unknowntrueunknownunknown
NOT Afalsetrueunknown

The surprising consequence: for a NULL area, AREA < 30 and NOT (AREA < 30) are both unknown, so neither rule draws the building. That is deliberate: the only way to catch the NULLs is to say so, with AREA IS NULL, or with the "else" rule below.

The "else" rule

A rule marked else: true applies to the features that no other rule of the same pass has drawn. Only the rules active at the map's scale count, and an "else" rule can have its own scale range:

- rules:
  - name: evere
    filter: ${MUNNISCODE = '21006'}
    symbolizers: [{polygon: {fill-color: '#E6A15C'}}]
  - name: autres
    else: true                          # every other municipality, and the NULLs
    symbolizers: [{polygon: {fill-color: '#E4E1DA'}}]

A reminder from chapter 25: it is not "the first rule that matches". All the rules whose filter is true draw the feature, in the order of the file. The "else" rule is the model's only "else".

Always a result

As in chapter 23, an error in a filter does not stop the rendering. An unreadable filter (${AREA << 30}) gives a warning with its line, and the rule is never applied. A field missing from the layer (${SURFACE > 30}) gives a warning with the list of available fields, and counts as NULL: the result is therefore unknown.

29. Read once, evaluate everywhere

A filter is a small text read only once, when the style is read. It is turned into a tree, then this tree is applied to every feature without ever reading the text again. It is the same principle as a programming language's interpreter, much smaller.

From text to tree, in two steps

  1. Cutting into words (the "lexer"). AREA >= 30 AND AREA < 100 becomes a series of 7 words: AREA, >=, 30, AND, AREA, <, 100. Spaces disappear, 30 is recognised as a number, '1140' as a text, AND as a keyword.
  2. Assembling (the "parser"). The words are arranged into a tree, by operator precedence: comparisons first, then NOT, then AND, and finally OR. It is the same rule as "multiplication before addition": a OR b AND c reads a OR (b AND c).
AREA >= 30 AND AREA < 100

            AND
          /     \
       >=         <
      /  \       /  \
   AREA   30  AREA   100

Field names become numbers

At the start of each pass, each field name in the tree is replaced by its column number in the layer: AREA becomes "column 2" for the buildings. Looking a name up among 17 fields for each of the 22,293 addresses would be pointless: it is done once per pass, and each evaluation reads the right column directly. That is also when a missing field is detected.

What it costs

Area classes over the whole Region
  1. 5 rules with a filter, 247,625 buildings: at most 5 × 247,625 = 1,238,125 evaluations.
  2. Each evaluation walks a tree of 3 to 7 nodes, with comparisons of numbers already read. Even counting generously, 100 nanoseconds per evaluation, that makes 1,238,125 × 100 ns ≈ 0.12 s for the whole Region.
  3. On a metatile at zoom 0, which holds a few thousand buildings, it is less than a millisecond.

That is negligible next to drawing. What can be expensive is not evaluating the filters, but reading the attributes: that is the subject of the next chapter. The report gives the real time, on a line of its own (filters … ms).

Measured on all the Evere addresses with the doors filter: 22,293 evaluations in 1.7 ms, i.e. 1.7 ms ÷ 22,293 ≈ 76 ns per evaluation, a little better than the generous 100 ns.

30. Read only the fields that matter

The Evere addresses file gives a striking example, and its numbers are exact to the byte:

The weight of geometries and attributes
  1. The .shp: a 100-byte header, then for each point an 8-byte record header and 20 bytes of content (the type and two doubles). 100 + 22,293 × 28 = 624,304 bytes, exactly the file's size.
  2. The .dbf: each address takes 794 bytes: 1 byte marking deleted records, then the sum of the reserved widths of its 17 fields, 793 bytes (88 for PARENTID, whereas 'https://databrussels.be/id/address/979654' uses only 41). With the 32 + 17 × 32 + 1 = 577-byte header and one end byte: 577 + 22,293 × 794 + 1 = 17,701,220 bytes, exactly the file's size.
  3. The attributes therefore weigh 17,701,220 ÷ 624,304 ≈ 28 times more than the geometries.

Until step 3a, render2d told GDAL nothing about the fields. So GDAL decoded all the attributes of every feature: the 17 fields of each address were converted into texts and numbers, then thrown away unused. Step 3b fixes this both ways, with SetIgnoredFields:

  • At the start of each pass, the fields named in the filters of the rules active at that scale are listed. For ${PARENTID IS NULL}, it is a single field.
  • GDAL is told to ignore all the others. It still reads the 794-byte record, but decodes only the 88 bytes of PARENTID: no conversion of the 16 others into text, no memory allocated for them.
  • A pass with no active filter asks for no field at all. GDAL then no longer needs the .dbf: the styles of step 3a even get a little faster.
Measured before and after, on a 500 m × 350 m square of Evere (601 addresses, 1:1,786), without style, median of 5 runs
  1. With the .qix: reading 1.7 ms at step 3a, 0.7 ms at step 3b. Almost only the geometries are left to read.
  2. Without an index: 5.7 ms, then 4.3 ms. The gain is smaller because, without an index, most of the time goes into walking through the 22,293 records of the .shp.
  3. Beware of the very first run: it gave 143.6 ms without an index, because the file was not yet in the operating system's cache. To compare two versions, repeat the measurement and keep the median.

Another optimisation is tempting: passing the filter to GDAL (SetAttributeFilter), so that it discards the features itself. Not at this step. GDAL's syntax (OGR SQL) is not exactly CQL, an "else" rule needs to see every feature, and with a shapefile GDAL must read every record anyway to test the filter. It will be useful with PostGIS, where the database can use its own indexes. Measure first.

33. From character to pixel step 3c

A label is text laid on the map. Drawing text means doing again, for each letter, what render2d already does for a polygon. A font holds, for each character, a glyph: its outline, described by segments and Bézier curves, exactly like a building with a courtyard in chapter 11. The "8" is a polygon with two holes.

  • TrueType (Apple, 1991) describes its outlines with quadratic Béziers (one control point per curve).
  • PostScript Type 1 (Adobe, 1984), and today OpenType's CFF, use cubic Béziers (two control points), the same as our circle in chapter 20.
  • The glyph is drawn on an abstract grid, the em, often of 1,000 or 2,048 units. For a 9-pixel text, the font is scaled so that the em is 9 pixels: again an affine transformation.
  • Then comes rasterisation: filling the outline, with the anti-aliasing of chapter 9. At these small sizes, hinting slightly adjusts the outlines to the pixel grid, so that the vertical strokes of a "1" fall on whole pixels and stay sharp.

34. Text with Cairo

Cairo offers two paths for text.

  • The "toy" API, that is its official name: cairo_select_font_face, cairo_set_font_size, cairo_text_path. Give it a font name and a UTF-8 text, and Cairo looks up the glyphs and lays them one after the other. No fine kerning, no ligatures, no fallback font if a character is missing.
  • Pango and HarfBuzz, GNOME's text layout libraries. HarfBuzz does the shaping: it chooses and places the glyphs according to the rules of each script (the kerning of "AV", the "fi" ligature, Arabic letters changing shape according to their position). Pango adds fallback fonts and line breaking.

For house numbers, the toy API is plenty: ten digits and a few letters. For street names with their accents ("Chaussée de Haecht"), it works too, since accented letters are glyphs in their own right in the font. Pango will become useful for names laid along a curved line and for non-Latin scripts. So we start with the toy API, behind our canvas: moving to Pango later will change only CairoCanvas.

Text is a path

cairo_text_path draws nothing: it adds the glyph outlines to the current path, like a moveTo and some curveTos. It can then be filled, stroked, or both. It is PostScript's charpath operator, and it is what makes the halo so simple (next chapter).

In a PDF or an SVG

Since we go through cairo_text_path, each letter becomes a drawn outline, exactly like a building: in PDF and SVG, house numbers stay sharp at every zoom, and the map does not depend on the fonts installed by whoever opens it. The price: it is no longer text, it can be neither selected nor searched in the PDF (checked with pdffonts: no embedded font). If one day "real" text is wanted, we would keep the outline for the halo and write the letters on top with cairo_show_text, which embeds a subset of the font.

Which font?

The font name (font-family) is resolved by the system: on Windows, Arial or Segoe UI always exist; on Linux, DejaVu Sans is almost always there. An unknown name gives a replacement font, without an error.

35. The text symbolizer

A new symbolizer joins polygon, line and point. As in GeoServer:

- name: numeros
  filter: ${PARENTID IS NULL}             # a door, not a box (chapter 31)
  scale: [min, 1100]                      # chapter 37
  symbolizers:
  - text:
      label: ${POLICENUM}                 # the text: a field's value
      font-family: Arial
      font-size: 9                        # in pixels
      fill-color: '#3A2A20'
      halo: {radius: 1.5, fill-color: '#FFFFFF'}
      anchor: [0.5, 0.5]                  # the text's centre on the point (SLD default: [0, 0.5])
      rotation: ${360 - CARTOANGLE}       # chapter 36

An expression that gives a value

The filter of chapter 28 answered true, false or unknown. label: ${POLICENUM} is an expression that gives a value: for the door of no. 106 rue de Paris, the text '106'. It is the same reader and the same tree as for filters, with one more node at the top. The text can also be written as is (label: Evere), or combine both later.

A NULL value gives no label: there is nothing to write. A number is written as expected: AREA = 120.5 gives "120.5", 120.0 gives "120".

The anchor

anchor: [x, y] says which point of the text sits on the map point, as a fraction of the text's box: [0, 0] the bottom-left corner, [0.5, 0.5] the centre, [1, 0.5] the middle of the right edge. For "106" at 9 pixels (18 px wide, 8 px high), [0.5, 0.5] places the text from 9 px left to 9 px right of the point, and from 4 px below to 4 px above. displacement: [dx, dy] then shifts it by a number of pixels.

The halo

A dark grey number on a brown building is hard to read. The halo is a light border around each letter, which lifts it from any background. Thanks to text-as-path, it is two operations on the same path:

  1. stroke the glyph outlines in white, with a width of 2 × the radius: for radius: 1.5, a 3-pixel stroke, 1.5 pixels of which spill outside each letter (and 1.5 inside, which will be covered);
  2. fill the glyphs in the text colour, on top.

With round joins (LineJoin::Round), the halo also hugs the points of the "7" without making spikes.

One exception to the rule of chapter 11: glyphs are filled with the non-zero rule, not even-odd. Fonts are designed for it: the hole of the "8" turns the other way from its outer outline, and two overlapping outlines in a composite glyph (an "é" whose accent touches the letter) must stay solid.

Labels above everything

An important decision here. If a label were drawn in its place in the layer order (chapter 25), a street name laid with the roads would then be covered by the buildings of the next layer. GeoServer, Mapnik and QGIS all do the same thing: labels are set aside while the layers are drawn, then drawn all together at the end, above the map. We do the same: one last pass, for all the layers. That is also where collisions will settle later: to decide that one label hides another, they must all be in front of us.

36. Rotation from CARTOANGLE

The UrbIS data give each address an angle, CARTOANGLE, meant to orient its number along the façade. But which way does it turn, and from where? We checked it on the data rather than guessing.

Rue de Paris, from no. 3 to no. 5
  1. No. 3: X = 152,205.359; Y = 174,186.769. No. 5: X = 152,209.229; Y = 174,183.429.
  2. From 3 to 5: dx = +3.870 m towards the east, dy = −3.340 m (towards the south).
  3. The direction, measured the mathematicians' way (from the east, anticlockwise): atan2(−3.340, 3.870) = −40.80°, i.e. −40.80 + 360 = 319.20°.
  4. CARTOANGLE is 319.32° for no. 3 and 319.33° for no. 5: within 0.1°, it is the direction of the street.

Over all of Evere's doors, the same measurement between each door and its nearest neighbour in the same street (5,743 pairs) gives:

No doubt: CARTOANGLE is measured from the east, anticlockwise. The remaining 7 % are street corners, where the nearest neighbour is already in the perpendicular street.

SLD turns the other way

In SLD, rotation is in degrees clockwise, as seen on the screen. The angle must therefore be reversed: rotation: ${360 - CARTOANGLE}. For no. 3: 360 − 319.32 = 40.68° clockwise, and the number slopes down to the right, along rue de Paris. (${-CARTOANGLE} would give the same drawing; 360 − x keeps a positive angle, easier to read in a report.)

This calls for something new in our expressions: arithmetic, +, −, × (*), ÷ (/), with the usual precedence (multiplication before addition). CQL provides for it, GeoServer accepts it, and it will serve filters too: ${AREA / 10000 > 1}, buildings of more than a hectare.

Text is not read upside down

An angle of 180° would turn the number over: "106" would become unreadable. The cartographers' rule: if the on-screen angle falls between 90° and 270°, add 180°. The text is then turned "right way up", and still follows the same direction. The UrbIS producers have already done this work: of Evere's 6,282 doors, only about fifteen have an angle in that range (for example 131.76° on avenue du Cimetière de Bruxelles). We apply the rule anyway, for all the data that have not received that care.

In the matrix

On screen, the y axis goes down (chapter 2): a "clockwise" rotation in the image's world is a positive-angle rotation with y pointing down. For each label, three simple transformations are composed: move the origin to the point (in pixels), rotate, then shift according to the anchor. They are matrices from chapter 5, applied to the glyph path. The map point itself still goes through our ground → pixels matrix.

37. At what scale a house number fits

Two neighbouring numbers along the same street must not overlap. So we must compare the room a number takes with the gap between two doors, both in pixels.

Evere's doors, measured
  1. The gap between a door and its nearest neighbour in the same street: median 6.1 m (half the doors between 5.5 m and 7.1 m). The classic Brussels façade.
  2. "106" at 9 pixels takes 18 pixels, plus 2 × 1.5 pixels of halo: 21 pixels.
  3. So 6.1 m must make at least 21 pixels: the resolution must be at most 6.1 ÷ 21 ≈ 0.29 m/px.
  4. As a scale: 0.29 ÷ 0.00028 ≈ 1:1,040. Rounded to scale: [min, 1100]: beyond that, the rule draws nothing.

Tile edges, free for now

A number placed 3 pixels from a metatile's edge spills over onto its neighbour. If the neighbour did not draw it, it would be cut off. This free join holds as long as every label is drawn no matter what. The day collisions are handled ("this number hides that one, keep only one"), two neighbouring tiles may make different decisions, and the edge problem will come back. That will be a later step, with its own notes.

39. The pixel ratio: high-density screens step 3c

A 4K screen has 3,840 dots across. So that text is not tiny, the operating system enlarges everything, for example to 150 %: a button meant for 100 pixels takes 150. There are therefore two units:

  • the screen dot, the real little lit square;
  • the logical pixel, the one the operating system and the browser measure things with. The browser calls their ratio devicePixelRatio: here 1.5.
A house number on a 4K screen at 150 %
  1. The map takes 850 logical pixels across. For a sharp image, viewer2d asks for 850 × 1.5 = 1,275 pixels: one image pixel per screen dot.
  2. The style says font-size: 9. Without correction, render2d draws the number over 9 image pixels, and the eye sees it at 9 ÷ 1.5 = 6 pixels. Too small.
  3. With --pixel-ratio 1.5, every length in the style is multiplied by 1.5: the number is drawn over 9 × 1.5 = 13.5 image pixels, and seen at 13.5 ÷ 1.5 = 9 pixels. Sharp, and the right size.

Everything the style gives in pixels follows the same rule: stroke widths and dashes, point sizes, fonts, halos, displacements. A 0.8-pixel outline becomes 1.2 pixels in the image.

The scale too

The scale (chapter 7) is computed on the pixels one sees. On a view of the whole Region at 3.847 m per image pixel: without a ratio, 3.847 ÷ 0.00028 ≈ 1:13,740; but a seen pixel covers 3.847 × 1.5 ≈ 5.77 m, i.e. 5.77 ÷ 0.00028 ≈ 1:20,600. That is the scale render2d uses with --pixel-ratio 1.5, so that scale: [min, 1100] rules apply when the number really fits on the screen (chapter 37).

It is Mapnik's scale factor, and the reason for the "@2x" tiles of modern base maps. Without the option, the ratio is 1: tiles2d, PDFs and SVGs do not change.

In the code

  • View::pixelRatio: scaleDenominator() is resolution × pixelRatio ÷ 0.00028.
  • scaledStyle(style, ratio) (render/symbolize): a copy of the style with every length multiplied. Applied once, when the style is loaded, default styles included.
  • The report says so: scale 1:525 (pixel ratio 1.5: 0.147 m per pixel seen).
  • viewer2d sends the browser's devicePixelRatio with each view, and adds it to the command.

From −1 … +1 to pixels

The SLD size (size) is the symbol's height in pixels. Our convention: the shape is inscribed in a square of size pixels per side, so the frame is multiplied by size ÷ 2, and the y axis is flipped (the image has y pointing down, chapter 2).

A 10-pixel triangle placed on image point (400, 300)

41. Rotation and size read from the attributes

As for labels, a symbol's rotation and size can be expressions (chapter 35): rotation: ${…}, size: ${…}. The same reader, the same tree, the same fields read only if they are used.

Orienting a triangle along the façade

A triangle with rotation 0 points up, to the north. SLD turns clockwise. CARTOANGLE is counted from the east, anticlockwise (chapter 36). For the tip to follow the façade:

Rue de Paris, no. 3: CARTOANGLE = 319.32°
  1. Pointing east means turning 90° clockwise from the north.
  2. Pointing in direction a (anticlockwise from the east) therefore means turning 90 − a clockwise.
  3. 90 − 319.32 = −229.32°, i.e. −229.32 + 360 = 130.68°: the tip heads south-east, down and to the right, the way rue de Paris goes down.
rotation: ${90 - CARTOANGLE}

One difference from labels: a symbol has no "upside down". A triangle pointing south-west must point south-west: the + 180° rule of chapter 36 applies only to text.

A trap found by trying: an equilateral triangle shows a direction poorly. Its three vertices look alike; rotated by 45°, it seems to point elsewhere. For the ground arrows of UrbIS Topo, a small arrow image (chapter 42), rotated the same way, reads without ambiguity: 0° east, 90° north, 45° north-east, checked on the image.

Proportional symbols

A circle whose size tells a quantity: the number of flats behind a door, the girth of a tree. The classic trap: if the diameter follows the value, a circle representing 4 times more has 4 times the diameter, hence 4 × 4 = 16 times the area. The eye compares areas, and sees 16 instead of 4.

The right calculation: the area follows the value
  1. A circle's area is proportional to the square of its diameter. For it to be proportional to the value, the diameter must be proportional to the square root of the value.
  2. A door with 1 flat: a 4-pixel circle. A door with 4 flats: 4 × √4 = 4 × 2 = 8 pixels (not 16). A tower with 100 flats: 4 × √100 = 40 pixels.
  3. In YSLD: size: ${4 * sqrt(N)}. It is our first CQL function, sqrt, to add to expressions.

Cartographers sometimes go further: the eye slightly underestimates large areas, and Flannery's correction uses an exponent of 0.57 instead of 0.5. We stick to the square root, the basic rule.

Pixels or metres: the unit of measure

All our sizes are in pixels: a 5-pixel circle stays a 5-pixel circle at every scale. That is what we want for a symbol. But a tree also has a real size: a crown 8 m across. SLD 1.1 provides a unit of measure per symbolizer (uom), and GeoServer writes it uom: metre in YSLD. The size is then in ground metres, and converts into pixels with the resolution:

An 8 m crown
  1. At zoom 2 (0.25 m/px): 8 ÷ 0.25 = 32 pixels.
  2. At zoom −2 (4 m/px): 8 ÷ 4 = 2 pixels, almost invisible.
  3. On a 150 % screen (chapter 39): nothing to correct. A size in metres is already a real size; only sizes in pixels are multiplied by the pixel ratio.

Both will be combined with scale ranges: the crown in metres close up, a small symbol in pixels from afar.

42. PNG images

A pictogram (a tree, a school, a station) is often a small image. SLD calls it an external graphic; in YSLD, as in GeoServer:

- point:
    size: 16
    symbols:
    - external: {url: icons/arbre.png, format: image/png}
  • The path is relative to the style file: icons/arbre.png next to arbres.yaml. The style and its images travel together.
  • The size (size) is the image's height on screen, in pixels; the width follows, to keep the proportions. Without size, the image keeps its natural size.
  • The centre of the image is placed on the point. Rotation works as for shapes.
  • A missing image blocks nothing: a warning, and the point is drawn with the next shape in symbols, if there is one. That is SLD's rule: take the first symbol we know how to draw.

With Cairo

Cairo reads PNGs by itself (cairo_image_surface_create_from_png), without any extra dependency. The image is read once per rendering, kept in memory, then laid at each point as a paint source, with a matrix that gives it the right size and place. Two details:

  • Transparency. A PNG has an alpha channel (the softened edge of a pictogram). Cairo converts it on reading into "premultiplied" alpha, the form it uses to blend colours: nothing to do on our side.
  • Blur. A 16 × 16 pixel icon displayed at 16 pixels is sharp. Displayed at 24 pixels, on a 150 % screen, it is enlarged and becomes blurred. The classic remedy: provide the icon at 32 × 32 and display it at 16; it stays sharp up to a pixel ratio of 2. Exactly what SVG will later make unnecessary.

In PDF and SVG, Cairo embeds the image as is: a raster image in a vector document. One more argument for SVG one day.

43. Hatching

Filling a polygon with a repeated pattern: oblique strokes, cross-hatching, dots. It is a classic of plans (hatched public land, a planned building) and SLD provides for it: a fill with a graphic fill. In YSLD, with GeoServer's "stroke" shapes:

- polygon:
    fill-graphic:
      size: 8                                   # an 8 × 8 pixel cell
      symbols:
      - mark: {shape: 'shape://slash', stroke-color: '#7A4A35', stroke-width: 1}
ShapeIn the cell
shape://slasha stroke from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner: "/"
shape://backslashthe other diagonal: "\"
shape://horline, shape://vertlinea horizontal, or vertical, stroke through the middle
shape://timesboth diagonals: cross-hatching
shape://dota small dot in the centre (a circle also works)

One cell, repeated

The pattern is drawn only once, in a small 8 × 8 pixel image, then Cairo repeats it like tiling (CAIRO_EXTEND_REPEAT) to fill the polygon. Two traps, and two worked examples:

1. Joining the diagonals
  1. The "/" stroke goes from corner (0, 8) to corner (8, 0) of the cell (y pointing down). With a 1-pixel width, it overflows by half a pixel on each side, and this overflow is clipped at the cell's edge.
  2. The strokes of the left and right neighbouring cells pass through our corners (0, 0) and (8, 8): their overflow should bite a little into our cell, but it was clipped in theirs. Result: a small notch at every join, every 8 pixels, along each hatch line.
  3. The remedy: also draw, in each cell, the stroke of the left neighbour and that of the right neighbour (from (−8, 8) to (0, 0), and from (8, 16) to (16, 8)). What sticks out of their strokes into our cell is exactly the pieces missing at corners (0, 0) and (8, 8).
2. Joining two tiles
  1. The tiling starts at the image's corner (0, 0). Two neighbouring 1,024-pixel metatiles therefore start their tiling 1,024 pixels apart.
  2. With an 8-pixel cell: 1,024 ÷ 8 = 128 whole cells, the hatching joins.
  3. With a 10-pixel cell: 1,024 ÷ 10 = 102.4 cells. There remains 1,024 − 102 × 10 = 4 pixels of offset: the hatching breaks at every tile edge.
  4. The remedy: anchor the tiling to the ground, not to the image. It is aligned on the Lambert 72 point (0, 0), converted into pixels. All images, all tiles, all views at the same level then share the same tiling, whatever the cell size.

It is the same spirit as the free join of labels (chapter 37): if every tile computes the same thing in the same place, the tiles join by themselves.

A third trap, found with real coordinates

The Lambert 72 point (0, 0) is very far from Brussels. For a map at 1:1,200 (0.33 m/px) around X = 150,500, it falls 150,500 ÷ 0.33 ≈ −450,000 pixels from the image's edge. Yet Cairo hands drawing over to pixman, which computes coordinates in "16.16" fixed point: 16 bits for the integer part, hence at most ± 32,768. Beyond that, the cell is placed anywhere, and the first attempt drew no hatching at all. Since the tiling repeats every n pixels, the origin is kept modulo n: −450,000 modulo 8 is a number between 0 and 8, and the tiling is exactly the same. The join test now uses real Brussels coordinates, so that the error does not come back.

Until now, render2d had a single client: its own command line. The six libraries can read a layer (r2d_gdal), read a style (r2d_style), draw a layer (r2d_render). But what makes them a map is written directly in main(), in app/render2d.cpp:

  1. open each source, with the SOURCE::layer syntax;
  2. read each style, scale it to the pixel ratio (§39), fall back on the default style if the file is bad;
  3. check that the layers have the same CRS;
  4. compute the total extent;
  5. draw the layers in order, with the default styles specific to each geometry type (§26);
  6. draw the labels of all the layers, on top (§35);
  7. build the report and the warnings.

A second client arrives: editor2d, the desktop viewer, which links render2d's libraries directly (see its own notes). It needs steps 1 to 7, but not the writing of a file. Rather than copy those 150 lines, we take them out of main() to make a seventh library, r2d_map.

Changing the shape without changing the result

This step adds no mapping feature. It moves code. Martin Fowler calls it refactoring: changing a program's structure without changing what it does. The rule that goes with it is simple: prove it. Before touching the code, the old render2d.exe is run on a few real commands, and its PNGs and reports are kept. Afterwards, the new one must produce the same bytes in the PNGs, and the same report apart from the version and the milliseconds (chapter 49).

46. What lives long, what changes with every image

The command line lives a few tenths of a second: everything is opened, used once, closed. editor2d stays open for hours and draws hundreds of views. The central question of Map is therefore: what is done once, and what is redone at every view?

WhenWhatIn Map
Once, on openingOpen the GDAL sources. Read and check the style files. Check the CRSs. Compute the three default styles of each layer.Map::open
When the pixel ratio changesRedo the scaled copies (§39), from the original styles, which are kept.setPixelRatio

At every viewSet the spatial filter, read the features, filter, draw, label, count.render

Why keep the original styles

Scaling a style multiplies all its lengths. Doing it again on an already scaled copy would multiply twice.

  1. The style says stroke-width: 0.8. Screen at 150 %: 0.8 × 1.5 = 1.2 image pixels.
  2. The window is moved to a 100 % screen, ratio 1. Starting from the copy: 1.2 × 1 = 1.2. Wrong: we wanted 0.8.
  3. One could "divide by the old ratio": 1.2 ÷ 1.5 × 1 = 0.8. But each round trip between two screens would add a small floating-point rounding error (§8), and the previous ratio would have to be remembered. Starting again from the original is simpler, and always exact.
  4. So we always start from the original: 0.8 × 1 = 0.8. Then, back on the 4K screen: 0.8 × 1.5 = 1.2. A round trip without drift.

The default colour does not move when a layer is hidden

The default styles depend on the layer's position (§26: parcels and buildings must not have the same colour). This position is the layer's among the opened layers, fixed on opening. Hiding layer 1 therefore does not move layer 2 "up" to position 1: it keeps its colour. A layer that is unticked disappears; the others do not change their look.

Open layers, and threads

An open GDAL source must be read by only one thread at a time. Map takes no lock: it is up to its user to use it from a single thread. editor2d does so by handing the whole Map to its render thread, its sole owner. The command line has only one thread: nothing to do.

48. A surface in memory: CairoImage

CairoTarget (§13) is made for files: it chooses PNG, SVG or PDF from the extension, and finish() writes the file. editor2d does not want a file: it wants the pixels, to send them to the graphics card. So a small class is added next to it, rather than complicating CairoTarget:

// src/backend/cairo/cairo_image.hpp
class CairoImage {
public:
    static std::unique_ptr<CairoImage> create(ImageSize size, std::string& error);
    Canvas&              canvas();
    ImageSize            size() const;
    int                  stride() const;    // bytes from one row to the next
    const unsigned char* pixels();          // flushes, then the first byte of row 0
};

ARGB32, byte by byte

It is the same surface as for a PNG: CAIRO_FORMAT_ARGB32. Each pixel is a 32-bit integer, alpha in the most significant byte, then red, green, blue. And the colours are premultiplied by alpha. On a PC (a "little-endian" processor), the least significant byte comes first in memory.

  1. A half-transparent orange pixel: red 255, green 128, blue 0, alpha 50 %.
  2. Alpha on 8 bits: 0.5 × 255 = 127.5, rounded to 128 (0x80).
  3. Premultiplied: R = 255 × 128 ÷ 255 = 128 (0x80); G = 128 × 128 ÷ 255 = 16,384 ÷ 255 = 64.25, i.e. 64 (0x40); B = 0.
  4. The integer: A, R, G, B = 0x80804000.
  5. In memory, least significant first: 00 40 80 80, that is B, G, R, A.

Demo — an ARGB32 pixel in memory

The stride and the flush

Rows are stored one after the other, row 0 at the top. The number of bytes from one row to the next, the stride, can be larger than width × 4: always ask Cairo for it (cairo_image_surface_get_stride), never assume it.

  1. A 1,000 × 800 pixel image: a useful row is 1,000 × 4 = 4,000 bytes. Cairo gives stride = 4,000.
  2. Pixel (x = 10, y = 3) starts at byte 3 × 4,000 + 10 × 4 = 12,000 + 40 = 12,040.
  3. The whole image: 800 × 4,000 = 3,200,000 bytes, about 3.1 MB.

Before reading these bytes, cairo_surface_flush must be called: Cairo may keep operations pending, and guarantees the memory's content only afterwards. pixels() always does it, so that it cannot be forgotten. It is also what cairo_surface_write_to_png does before compressing: a CairoImage and a PNG CairoTarget of the same size, after the same drawing, have exactly the same pixels. A test checks it.

Michael Feathers calls a characterisation test a test that does not say what the program should do, but what it does today, to check that a transformation has not changed it. Exactly our need:

  1. Before the change: with the current render2d.exe, run a few commands on real data (several layers, a style with filters and labels, a deliberately missing layer, a --pixel-ratio 1.5). Keep the PNGs and the reports in a regress/before/ folder.

The FID: a feature's name

To select a feature, we must be able to designate it, and find it again later. GDAL gives each feature an identifier, the FID (feature id): an integer, unique in its layer. For a FlatGeobuf or a shapefile, it is its rank in the file; for a PostGIS table, its primary key. With it, a precise feature can be asked of GDAL again (GetFeature(fid)) without rereading the layer.

So far, FeatureSource::forEach passed the geometry and attributes to the visitor, but not the FID: render2d did not need it to draw. The visitor now receives all three:

using FeatureVisitor = std::function<void(const Geometry&, const Attributes&, std::int64_t fid)>;

It is an interface change, small but everywhere: GdalLayer, renderLayer (which ignores it), the in-memory sources of the tests. Rendering does not change by a single pixel: the characterisation test checks it, as for 0.3.5.

51. The same code to draw and to query

editor2d's rule is "only what is seen can be selected". A feature is visible when:

  1. its layer is displayed;
  2. a rule of its style is active at this scale (§24);
  3. that rule's filter is true for it (§28), or it is an else rule and no other applies.

renderLayer already computes points 2 and 3, feature by feature, just before drawing. If the query redid that computation on its own, the two would end up diverging: one day, a feature would be selectable without being drawn, or the reverse. So we extract that piece of renderLayer into a small class, RuleMatcher, used by both:

What is seen, exactly

  • A 20 m² building under an AREA >= 30 rule is not drawn: it is not selectable.
  • At 1:27,778, the addresses layer has no active rule: the click goes "through", and the building underneath answers.
  • At 1:977, addresses are drawn only by their number (a text rule): an address is selected by clicking on its number (§52).

52. Touching a geometry

GDAL's spatial filter works only with bounding rectangles: it returns the features whose rectangle touches our small square. An L-shaped building can have its rectangle under the click without the click being on the building. So an exact test is needed, per geometry type.

The tolerance, in pixels

A hand never clicks to the pixel, and a 5-pixel point is a tiny target. A distance of 4 pixels is accepted, translated into metres with the view's resolution. It therefore follows the eye, not the ground:

  1. At 0.182 m/px (a street, 1:977 at 150 %): 4 × 0.182 = 0.73 m.
  2. At 5.185 m/px (the Region, 1:27,778): 4 × 5.185 = 20.7 m.

The symbol's size is added to this tolerance: a point is touched by its circle, a line by its stroke.

A point: the distance

  1. A door drawn as a 5-pixel circle, at 150 %: 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 image pixels, i.e. a radius of 3.75 pixels.
  2. With the tolerance: 3.75 + 4 = 7.75 pixels. At 0.5 m/px: 7.75 × 0.5 = 3.875 m.
  3. The click is 3 m east and 2 m north of the door: distance √(3² + 2²) = √(9 + 4) = √13 ≈ 3.61 m.
  4. 3.61 ≤ 3.875: the door is touched.

An address number (a text rule) is touched the same way, around its anchor point, with a radius of half a character height: 9 × 1.5 ÷ 2 = 6.75 pixels, plus the tolerance.

A line: the distance to a segment

For a segment from A to B, the clicked point P is projected onto line AB, the projection is brought back between A and B, and the distance is measured.

  1. A = (0, 0), B = (10, 0), in metres, counted from A. Click at P = (4, 1.2).
  2. Position of the projection along AB: t = (AP · AB) ÷ |AB|² = (4 × 10 + 1.2 × 0) ÷ (10 × 10) = 40 ÷ 100 = 0.4. Between 0 and 1: the projection falls on the segment.
  3. Nearest point: A + 0.4 × AB = (4, 0). Distance: √((4 − 4)² + (1.2 − 0)²) = 1.2 m.
  4. Threshold at 0.25 m/px, for a 2-pixel stroke at 150 % (3 image pixels): (4 + 3 ÷ 2) × 0.25 = 5.5 × 0.25 = 1.375 m.
  5. 1.2 ≤ 1.375: the line is touched. (If t fell outside [0, 1], the distance to A or to B would be measured.)

A polyline is the minimum over all its segments.

A polygon: inside, or near the edge

A filled polygon is touched if the click is inside. To find out, a ray is drawn from the point to the right, and we count how many times it crosses an edge, all rings together: odd, inside; even, outside. It is the even-odd rule of §11, the one Cairo uses to fill: exactly what is painted is selected, inner courtyards included.

  1. A 10 m square building, from (0, 0) to (10, 10), with a courtyard from (4, 4) to (6, 6).
  2. Click at (2, 5). The ray y = 5, to the right, crosses the courtyard's left edge (x = 4), its right edge (x = 6), the building's right edge (x = 10): 3 crossings, odd: inside.
  3. Click at (5, 5), in the courtyard: it crosses x = 6 and x = 10: 2, even: outside. The courtyard is not painted, it does not select the building…
  4. … except near a wall: within the tolerance of an edge (the distance to segments, as for a line), the polygon is touched too. A building can be caught by its outline, even from its courtyard.

A polygon without fill (only an outline in the style) is touched only by its outline: that is all one sees of it.

54. An area instead of a point step 3g

§50 answered "what is here?". editor2d asks a broader question: "what is in this area?". The area is a rectangle dragged with the mouse, or a polygon clicked vertex by vertex. For render2d, both are one: a ring of points in ground coordinates. A rectangle is just a four-vertex polygon.

 area (ring, in metres) ──► its bounding rectangle                for GDAL: §18, the spatial filter
                        ──► the features VISIBLE at this scale    §51: RuleMatcher, as for the click
                        ──► those really touching the area        §55: the exact test
                        ──► layer by layer, from top to bottom

The path is the click's, with two differences.

1. The geometry is tested, not the symbol

For a click, what is seen is touched: a door's circle, a stroke's width, plus 4 pixels of tolerance (§52). For an area, the feature's geometry is taken, without symbol or tolerance. It is the usual meaning in GIS ("select by rectangle", "by polygon" in QGIS) and of the OGC intersects relation: a feature is taken if it has at least one point in common with the area.

What does not change: only the features drawn at this scale can answer. An inactive rule, a false filter: the feature is invisible, so not taken, even inside the area (§51).

2. We count

A click touches two or three features. An area can touch a hundred thousand: all of Brussels, at 1:27,778, in one rectangle. Each feature taken is copied, with its geometry and all its fields:

  1. An UrbIS building: about thirty vertices, at 16 bytes (two 8-byte numbers): 30 × 16 = 480 bytes.
  2. Its fields (INSPIRE identifiers of 50 to 60 characters, area…): say 300 bytes. In all, about 800 bytes.
  3. The Region's 143,729 buildings: 143,729 × 800 = 114,983,200 bytes ≈ 115 MB, and a second of reading (editor2d's notes, §8: 421 ms for reading alone).

The query therefore gets a cap (maxHits). One feature over the cap, and it copies nothing more and says so (truncated). Reading goes on to the end of the layer (GDAL cannot stop in the middle of a forEach), but memory stays bounded. The caller chooses the cap and what to do with the refusal: editor2d offers 10,000 features, the command line has no limit by default.

The three cases

The area Z is a polygon. It may even cross itself, like a bow tie: "inside Z" is then decided by the even-odd rule (§52), and both wings are inside. A feature E touches Z if:

  • E is a point: the point is in Z. It is the ray of §52, with Z as the polygon.
  • E is a line: one of its vertices is in Z, or one of its segments crosses an edge of Z. The second case is needed: a long street can cross a small rectangle without having a single vertex inside.
  • E is a polygon: the line's two tests, on all its rings, or Z is entirely inside E. This third case is that of a small rectangle dragged inside a large parcel: no parcel vertex in Z, no crossing edge, and yet the parcel contains the area. It is tested by asking whether Z's first vertex is inside E (even-odd, §52).

An area dragged entirely inside a building's courtyard does not take the building: the courtyard is not painted (§52), Z's first vertex is not "inside" the building. Always the same rule: only what is seen is taken.

Before these tests, the bounding rectangles are compared: if E's does not touch Z's, there is no need to go further. That is the case of most of the features GDAL returns around an L-shaped polygon.

55. Do two segments cross?

The whole test of §54 rests on one question: does segment AB (an edge of the feature) cross segment CD (an edge of the area)? It is answered without computing the crossing point, with a single repeated operation: the orientation of three points.

Left or right?

Standing at A, looking towards B. Is point C to the left, to the right, or in line? The sign of a small calculation says so:

orient(A, B, C) = (Bx − Ax) × (Cy − Ay) − (By − Ay) × (Cx − Ax)

   > 0: C is left of AB          < 0: right          = 0: on line AB

(It is the cross product of AB and AC, the same one that gives a ring's direction in §11.)

The rule

AB and CD cross if C and D are on either side of AB, and A and B are on either side of CD. Both are needed: the first alone only says that CD crosses the line AB, perhaps beyond B.

  1. A = (0, 0), B = (10, 0): a building edge, in metres. C = (4, −3), D = (6, 5): an edge of the area.
  2. orient(A, B, C) = (10 − 0) × (−3 − 0) − (0 − 0) × (4 − 0) = 10 × (−3) − 0 = −30: C on the right.
  3. orient(A, B, D) = (10 − 0) × (5 − 0) − (0 − 0) × (6 − 0) = 50 − 0 = 50: D on the left. Opposite signs: C and D are on either side of AB.
  4. orient(C, D, A) = (6 − 4) × (0 − (−3)) − (5 − (−3)) × (0 − 4) = 2 × 3 − 8 × (−4) = 6 + 32 = 38.
  5. orient(C, D, B) = (6 − 4) × (0 − (−3)) − (5 − (−3)) × (10 − 4) = 2 × 3 − 8 × 6 = 6 − 48 = −42. Opposite signs: A and B are on either side of CD.
  6. Both conditions are true: the segments cross.

Let us move the area's edge 8 m east: C = (12, −3), D = (14, 5).

  1. orient(A, B, C) = 10 × (−3) − 0 × 12 = −30; orient(A, B, D) = 10 × 5 − 0 × 14 = 50. Still on either side of the line AB.
  2. orient(C, D, A) = (14 − 12) × (0 + 3) − (5 + 3) × (0 − 12) = 2 × 3 − 8 × (−12) = 6 + 96 = 102.
  3. orient(C, D, B) = 2 × 3 − 8 × (10 − 12) = 6 − 8 × (−2) = 6 + 16 = 22. Same sign: A and B are on the same side of CD.
  4. No crossing. Indeed, CD cuts the axis y = 0 at x = 12 + 2 × 3 ÷ 8 = 12.75: beyond B (x = 10).

Edge cases: touching is crossing

When an orientation is 0, a point is exactly on the other segment's line. If it is also between its two ends (checked with the bounding rectangles), the segments touch. For intersects, touching counts: a building whose wall follows exactly the rectangle's edge is taken. With coordinates in metres and areas dragged with the mouse, this case is rare, but it must have an answer, and this one is the OGC's.

What does it cost?

  1. A rectangle has 4 edges; a building, about thirty. The worst case compares every edge with every edge: 30 × 4 = 120 pairs, 4 orientations each, i.e. 480 orientations.
  2. One orientation: two multiplications and three subtractions. Say 2 nanoseconds with memory access.
  3. 10,000 buildings in the area: 10,000 × 480 × 2 ns = 9,600,000 ns ≈ 10 ms, at worst. Most stop well before: one first vertex in the area, and it is done.
  4. A 20-vertex polygon instead of a rectangle: 5 times more edges, at most 50 ms. GDAL reading of the same features (editor2d's §8: about 3 µs per building, i.e. 30 ms) stays of the same order.

57. --config: the map described in a file step 3h

One usually works for a long time on a fixed set of data. Typing four sources and their four styles every time is long, and a typo in a path gives another map without anyone noticing. Yet the map is already described in a file: viewer2d's viewer.yaml, which editor2d reads too (§13 of its notes).

What render2d reads in it:

  • layers: the sources and their styles, in order (the first at the bottom);
  • start: bbox: the area to draw, if --bbox is not given;

One reader for two programs

This reader already existed: editor2d's config/Config.cpp (§13 of its notes). It moves into render2d, next to r2d_map, and editor2d uses it from there. Two programs, a single piece of code reading the file: they cannot understand the same viewer.yaml in two ways. (It is the rule of step 3f, "the same code to draw and to query", applied to configuration.)

Relative paths: from the file, not from where one stands

A path written in the file is read from the file's folder. Otherwise, the same command would give another map depending on the folder it is run from.

  1. The file: D:/work/viewer2d/viewer.yaml. Its folder: D:/work/viewer2d.
  2. It contains style: ../render2d/styles/parcelles.yaml.
  3. Joined: D:/work/viewer2d/../render2d/styles/parcelles.yaml.
  4. ".." goes up one folder: viewer2d/.. cancels out. What remains: D:/work/render2d/styles/parcelles.yaml.

Kept as is: a full path (C:/data/…), a connection (PG:…). And file.gpkg::batiments: only the part before :: is a path.

The command line has the last word

  • extra SOURCEs are added above the file's layers: --config viewer.yaml arbres.fgb --style arbres.yaml draws the file's map, plus the trees on top;
  • --size, --pixel-ratio, -o, --at, --box… work as without a file.

59. Writing YSLD step 3j

So far, render2d only read styles (§23). editor2d's legend dialog (its notes, §41 to §44) modifies them in the model (Style, §22) and must write them back. The writer sits next to the reader: a single place in the project knows YSLD.

std::string writeYsld(const Style& style);

What it writes

  • Always the same shape, in the model's order: name, feature-styles, rules, then for each rule name, scale, filter or else, symbolizers.
  • Only what differs from the default value: a 1-pixel stroke with a miter join is written as stroke-color alone. The file stays short and readable.
  • Colours as '#RRGGBB', with opacity separate when it is not 1; expressions between ${ }, with their original text (the model keeps it).
  • Scale ranges as [min, 15000], as written by hand.

What it loses

The comments and layout of a hand-written file: the model does not keep them (editor2d's decision, §33). editor2d copies the file to .bak before overwriting it.

The proof: the round trip

  1. Read batiments.yaml: model A.
  2. Write A: a text T.
  3. Read T again: model B.
  4. A and B must be equal, field by field; and a map drawn with A and a map drawn with B, identical byte for byte.

The test makes this round trip for every style of the styles/ folder: parcels, buildings, rotated numbers, symbols, hatching. A field forgotten by the writer shows up immediately.

Changing an open layer's style

bool Map::setLayerStyle(int i, const std::string& ysld, std::vector<std::string>& warnings);

The text is read (with its warnings), then scaled to the pixel ratio (§39); the GDAL source stays open. An unreadable text leaves the old style in place, and says so.

60. The property catalogue step 3k

editor2d's legend dialog gets a "+" (its notes, §45): on a symbolizer, it offers what can still be added. Who knows what a symbolizer can contain? The reader (§23) and the writer (§59) know, each in its own way. Rather than a third copy in editor2d, render2d says it: a catalogue, next to them.

struct PropertySpec {
    std::string key;     // "stroke", "hatch", "halo", "rotation"...
    std::string label;   // for menus: "Stroke", "Hatch"...
};
const std::vector<PropertySpec>& optionalProperties(SymbolizerKind kind);

void removeProperty(Symbolizer& s, const std::string& key);
Symbolizer newSymbolizer(SymbolizerKind kind, int colour);  // the default style's look (§46)

Optional or always there

A property is optional when its absence changes the drawing: a polygon without stroke has no outline. A property that always has a value (a point's size, a text's font) is not in the catalogue: it is modified, not added.

SymbolizerOptional properties
polygonfill, stroke, hatch, dashes

pointrotation, size by field, size in metres

The starting value

addProperty sets a value that shows: a dark grey (#333333) 1-pixel stroke, a 1.5-pixel white halo, 4 4 dashes, a 45° hatch every 8 pixels, a rotation of 0. newSymbolizer takes the default style's look (§46) in the layer's colour.

Adding "stroke" to a polygon, then writing it
  1. Before: polygon: {fill-color: '#C98B6B'}. hasProperty(s, "stroke") returns false.
  2. addProperty(s, "stroke"): the stroke gets colour #333333 and width 1. hasProperty returns true.
  3. writeYsld writes stroke-color: '#333333' alone: width 1 is the default value (§59), no need to write it.
  4. Read back, the model does have a stroke: the round trip holds.

The proof

For every symbolizer and every property of the catalogue: start from a newSymbolizer, add, write, read back → present; remove, write, read back → absent. A property added to the catalogue without the writer knowing it makes the test fail.

62. Reading the values, cutting into classes step 3m

editor2d's legend dialog gets two assistants (its notes, §48 to §51): Categorize (one colour per value of a field) and Graduate (classes of numbers, from light to dark). The work is done here, in three pieces that know nothing of ImGui.

1. What a field contains

struct FieldSummary {
    std::vector<std::pair<std::string, int>> distinct;  // value, count; sorted

One read of the whole layer, with only the requested field (§30). It takes a FeatureSource, not a GDAL layer: the test gives it an in-memory column, editor2d a GdalLayer. The geometry is still read with it; if that is slow on a large layer, GDAL will be taught to skip it. 100,000 buildings, 8 bytes per area: 800 KB of sorted numbers, nothing heavy.

2. The breaks

enum class BreakMethod { EqualIntervals, Quantiles };
std::vector<double> classBreaks(const std::vector<double>& sorted, int n, BreakMethod method);
editor2d §49's ten areas: 30, 45, 60, 80, 110, 150, 220, 300, 450, 900; 3 classes
  1. Equal intervals: (900 − 30) ÷ 3 = 290; the inner breaks 30 + 290 = 320 and 320 + 290 = 610.
  2. Quantiles: ranks ⌊1 × 10 ÷ 3⌋ = 3 and ⌊2 × 10 ÷ 3⌋ = 6, counting from 0: 80 and 220.
  3. Two equal breaks (many identical values) make only one, and a break equal to the smallest value is removed: fewer classes, never an empty class.
  4. Equal-interval breaks are rounded to 6 significant digits: 607.24 and not 607.2399999999999, the computer's binary writing.

categorize and graduate copy a model symbolizer into each rule, change its colour, write the filter, and end with a grey else rule. The filters are ordinary expressions (§28): the test reads them back with the parser, then checks that each of the ten areas falls into a single class.

AREA < 320          AREA >= 320 AND AREA < 610          AREA >= 610          else

A class's colour: rampColour(from, to, t), each component at fraction t of the way. From #FFF5EB to #8C2D04, halfway: (198, 145, 120) = #C69178 (the full calculation is in editor2d's §49).

65. Rasters: reading just what is seen step 4a

So far, render2d knows only features: points, lines, polygons, each with its attributes. A raster is a grid of pixels laid on the ground: an orthophoto, a terrain model, a hillshade, an old scanned map. GDAL reads them too (it is even its original trade); Cairo can lay down an image (§42). The path between the two is missing.

Where each pixel is: the geotransform

A GeoTIFF says where it lies with six numbers, the geotransform: the top-left corner, and a pixel's size. For a 2 km orthophoto tile at 25 cm:

x0 = 150,000   (the left edge, in Lambert 72 metres)      dx = 0.25   (25 cm to the right per column)
y0 = 171,000   (the top edge)                             dy = −0.25  (25 cm DOWN per row)
8,000 columns × 8,000 rows = 2,000 m × 2,000 m

It is the same idea as render2d's view (§4), in reverse: there, we went from the ground to the pixel of the image to produce; here, from the ground to the file's pixel.

A real file: UrbIS DSM tile 147171
  1. The TIFF does not contain its position: it is in the small file UrbDSM_2021_147171.tfw next to it (a world file), six lines: 0.5; 0; 0; −0.5; 147,000.384; 171,999.822.
  2. The world file gives the centre of the first pixel; GDAL, its corner. A pixel is 0.5 m: we step back half a pixel, 147,000.384 − 0.25 = 147,000.134, and 171,999.822 + 0.25 = 172,000.072. That is indeed what GDAL reports.
  3. Getting the convention wrong would shift the whole image by 25 cm in x and in y: under the UrbIS buildings, at 1:200, it would show.
  4. 2,000 × 2,000 pixels of 0.5 m: the tile covers 1 km × 1 km, from x ≈ 147,000 to 148,000 and from y ≈ 171,000 to 172,000.
The view: x from 150,500 to 151,500, y from 169,800 to 170,400, a 2,000 × 1,200 pixel image
  1. The view's resolution: (151,500 − 150,500) ÷ 2,000 = 0.5 m per image pixel.
  2. The left edge's column, in the file: (150,500 − 150,000) ÷ 0.25 = 2,000; the right edge's: (151,500 − 150,000) ÷ 0.25 = 6,000. The window is 4,000 columns.
  3. The top edge's row: (171,000 − 170,400) ÷ 0.25 = 2,400 (counted from the top: y0 − y); the bottom edge's: (171,000 − 169,800) ÷ 0.25 = 4,800. The window is 2,400 rows.
  4. We ask GDAL: "the window (2,000, 2,400) of 4,000 × 2,400 pixels, returned as 2,000 × 1,200": half, in each direction.

A view falling between two file pixels (an edge at 150,500.1 m) gives a fractional column: 2,000.4. GDAL accepts a fractional window (GDALRasterIOExtraArg), and that matters: rounding would shift the image by a few centimetres under the strokes, and it would show at 1:200.

Overviews: never read 14 GB for a thumbnail

The whole Region as a 25 cm orthophoto, overall view
  1. The Region is about 19 km wide: 19,000 ÷ 0.25 = 76,000 columns, and about 60,000 rows.
  2. Reading everything, in red, green, blue: 76,000 × 60,000 × 3 bytes ≈ 13.7 GB, for an image 2,000 pixels wide.
  3. On screen, a pixel covers 19,000 ÷ 2,000 = 9.5 m: 38 file pixels in each direction, 1,444 pixels read to keep one.

The remedy is classic: overviews, reduced copies stored in the file: at 50 cm, 1 m, 2 m, 4 m, 8 m… GDAL picks the right one by itself when the requested window is smaller than the piece read. In the overall view, the 8 m overview gives 2,375 × 1,875 pixels, i.e. 13 MB: a thousand times less.

  • A GeoTIFF without overviews gets them with gdaladdo -r average ortho.tif 2 4 8 16 32 64, once and for all.
  • A COG (Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF) already has them, arranged to be read in pieces, even remotely: the format of choice for the future (and for the WMS of step 4c).
  • Without overviews, render2d says so in a warning, with the command to run: a slow raster must not stay a mystery. Only beyond 4,096 pixels per side: the DSM tile (2,000 × 2,000, 16 MB) is read whole in 0.15 s, overviews would gain almost nothing.

As for vectors (§18), only the view's window is read, and GDAL reduces while reading (the average of the grouped pixels): the image arrives at the right size, it only remains to lay it down.

Which image pixels? Those whose centre falls on the raster

The raster's edge rarely falls exactly between two image pixels. A pixel straddling it: paint it or not? The rule: look at its centre. If it is on the raster, the pixel is painted; otherwise, not.

This is what matters for tiles: the Brussels ortho comes in 1 km tiles side by side (147171, 148171…). The image pixel overlapping the seam belongs to only one of the two tiles: no white line (nobody would have painted it), no darker line (painted twice, with an opacity).

Then, two cases:

  • Enlarging (an image pixel is smaller than a file pixel): take the file pixel under the centre, the "nearest neighbour". No blur: at 1:200, the DSM pixels show as sharp squares, and that is honest, it is its real precision.
  • Reducing: GDAL averages the file pixels under each image pixel, with the fractional window. Except for a palette: the average of indices 3 (forest) and 7 (water) would give 5 (road)! There, the nearest is kept.
The test's checkerboard, shifted by half a square
  1. The checkerboard: 4 squares of 10 m, from x = 1,000 to 1,040. The view: x from 1,005 to 1,045, 8 pixels of 5 m.
  2. The pixel centres: 1,007.5; 1,012.5; 1,017.5; …; 1,042.5.
  3. Pixel 0, centre 1,007.5: square 0 (from 1,000 to 1,010). Pixels 1 and 2, centres 1,012.5 and 1,017.5: square 1. The boundary x = 1,010 falls between pixels 0 and 1, to the pixel.
  4. Pixel 7, centre 1,042.5: beyond 1,040, outside the checkerboard, not painted. The block read is 7 pixels wide, not 8.

66. Rasters: laying the image down

From bands to Cairo pixels

GDAL returns bands: one grid per colour. Cairo wants premultiplied ARGB32 pixels (§48), in B, G, R, A order in memory. Depending on what the file holds:

The fileExampleWhat we do with it
3 bands, red, green, bluean orthophotoR, G, B as is; A = 255
4 bands, the 4th is an alphaan ortho clipped to the RegionR, G, B, A; premultiplied colours
3 bands of numbers (not bytes)a 16-bit satellite imageeach band stretched from its minimum to its maximum, like grey
1 band with a palettea map scanned in 256 colourseach value → its colour in the file's palette
1 band of numbersa hillshade (0 to 255), a terrain or surface model (in metres)in grey, from the smallest to the largest of the whole layer (later: a colour scale from the style)

A "no data" value (nodata, often −9999 in a terrain model, −3.4 × 1038 in the UrbIS DSM, or the black around a clipped ortho) becomes transparent: what is underneath shows.

A single mechanism for everything transparent: GDAL's mask. GDAL builds it by itself from what the file says, nodata, alpha band or embedded mask, and returns it as one more band: 255 = visible, 0 = transparent. render2d reads the mask with the colours and uses it as alpha; it need not know the three cases.

A reduction next to a nodata (the test)
  1. The checkerboard reduced to 2 × 2 pixels: each image pixel covers 2 × 2 squares. The bottom-right one covers red squares 140, 190, 140 and the "no data" square.
  2. One might think: (140 + 190 + 140 + 0) ÷ 4 = 117.5, a pixel darkened by the nodata. GDAL does better: it leaves the nodata aside, (140 + 190 + 140) ÷ 3 = 156.7 → 157.
  3. And the pixel is fully visible: three quarters of data are enough to make a pixel. The edge of a tile or of a clipped ortho does not darken when zooming out.
The UrbIS DSM in grey: from 13.0 m to 99.6 m
  1. The tile's lowest point, 13.0 m (the ground in a hollow), becomes black (0); the highest, 99.6 m (a roof), white (255).
  2. A roof at 40 m: (40 − 13.0) ÷ (99.6 − 13.0) = 27 ÷ 86.6 = 0.312; × 255 = 79.5 → 80, a dark grey.
  3. Why the minimum and maximum of the whole layer, and not of the view? With the view's, each image would have its own stretch: two neighbouring tiles2d tiles (§37) would show the same roof in two different greys, and the seam would show. Read once on opening (GDAL's statistics), they are the same everywhere.
An orthophoto pixel (R, G, B) = (255, 200, 100), an opacity of 50 %
  1. Without opacity: A = 255; in memory B, G, R, A = 100, 200, 255, 255.
  2. With 50 %: A = 255 × 0.5 = 127.5 → 128.
  3. Premultiplied (§48): each colour × 128 ÷ 255: R = 255 × 128 ÷ 255 = 128; G = 200 × 128 ÷ 255 = 100.4 → 100; B = 100 × 128 ÷ 255 = 50.2 → 50.
  4. In memory: 50, 100, 128, 128. Laid on white, Cairo computes: colour + (1 − 128/255) × white, i.e. R = 128 + 127 = 255, G = 100 + 127 = 227, B = 50 + 127 = 177: the photo, half faded.

A raster's style

GeoServer's YSLD has a symbolizer for this: raster. We take its most useful part first, opacity:

name: ortho
feature-styles:
- rules:
  - scale: [min, 20000]          (beyond that, the photo becomes mush: it is not shown)
    symbolizers:
    - raster:
        opacity: 0.6

Rules, scales and editor2d's legend dialog work as for vectors; filters make no sense (a raster has no attributes). Without a style: opacity 1. Later: color-map, a colour scale for a terrain model (hypsometric tints: green at the bottom, brown, white at the top).

In its place in the stack

A raster is a layer like the others: drawn at its rank (§25), usually at the very bottom. Vector layers are drawn on top, like a tracing over the photo. An ortho above the buildings would hide them: allowed (with an opacity, even useful), but rarely wanted.

The same coordinate system, to begin with. A raster in another system than the map (a satellite image in UTM) is refused with a clear warning: laying it down requires reprojecting it, which will be step 4b, together with vectors. A raster without a declared system (the UrbIS DSM: a TIFF and its world file, which gives only the position) is assumed to be in the map's, with a warning, like a vector without a system (§46).

A discreet change on the way: the map's system was the first layer's. With the DSM at the bottom (no system) and UrbIS above (Lambert 72), the map would have had no system. It is now the first declared system: Lambert 72, as expected.

What does not work on a raster, and why: "What is here?" (§50) and area selection ignore it, it has no features; selection by attributes (§64) answers "is a raster: it has no attributes".

What render2d displays

For a raster, the report line counts not features but pixels: those of the image, and those of the file they cover. On a DSM tile (the layer's line is split in two here, to fit the page):

render2d reads PostGIS through GDAL's PostgreSQL driver, without any code of its own (see editor2d's notes, §63, for the connection, the tests and the password). This chapter is a cheat sheet: the mechanics of Oracle Spatial and their PostGIS equivalents, ogr2ogr recipes, checks in psql.

The source, in render2d and editor2d

The password: in pgpass.conf (%APPDATA%\postgresql\pgpass.conf on Windows, ~/.pgpass elsewhere), never in the string nor in the yaml. One line: db.example.org:5432:gis:gisuser:PASSWORD.

A database's tables: listLayers("PG:…") (render2d 0.4.1) returns each layer with its name (schema.table outside public), its type, its feature count and its system, without the extent (costly on a large table). editor2d's "Connect to database" dialog calls it (its notes, §65), in a separate thread. Since 0.4.3, it also says whether a layer has a geometry (a GeoPackage stores value lists in tables without geometry) and gives the feature count only if GDAL knows it without counting (-1 otherwise).

The password typed in that dialog: setSessionPassword(connection, password) (render2d 0.4.2) keeps it in memory, attached to the connection; every GDAL opening of that connection gets password=… added, and nothing else sees it (the source, the yaml, the messages). The PGPASSWORD variable, tried first, was not seen by libpq.dll on Windows.

Decoding one and the other
  1. GTYPE 2003: 2 = two dimensions, 0 = no measure (LRS), 03 = polygon. A multipolygon (Ixelles) would be 2007.
  2. ELEM_INFO (1, 1003, 1), one triplet per ring: the first ring starts at the 1st ordinate; 1003 = outer ring (2003 = a hole); 1 = straight segments (2 = arcs, 3 = rectangle given by its two corners). Ixelles in two pieces: (1, 1003, 1, 11, 1003, 1), the second starts at the 11th ordinate (5 points × 2 + 1).
  3. In PostGIS, none of that to write: the text says "POLYGON" and its parentheses make the rings. In the database, it becomes EWKB, 97 bytes:
01                    byte order: 1 = little-endian (PC)
03000020              type 3 = Polygon, plus the 0x20000000 flag: a SRID follows
8a7a0000              SRID 0x7A8A = 31,370
01000000              1 ring
05000000              5 points
0000000020fb0141 ...  147,300 as a double (8 bytes), then 171,300, etc.
                      5 points × 16 bytes = 80 bytes
1 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 80 = 97 bytes. That is what GDAL receives: the Simple Features WKB (§15), with the SRID added.

A difference to keep in mind: Oracle wants the outer ring anticlockwise, and holes the other way. PostGIS accepts both directions (ST_ForcePolygonCCW to impose Oracle's). render2d fills with even-odd (§11): direction does not matter to it.

ogr2ogr: the recipes

ogr2ogr is GDAL's Swiss army knife: one source into another, with a filter, a reprojection, a sort. The form: ogr2ogr -f TARGET_FORMAT TARGET SOURCE [layer].

# a shapefile into PostGIS
ogr2ogr -f PostgreSQL "PG:host=HOST dbname=gis user=USER" \
  UrbISBuildings_04000_Buildings.shp \
  -nln buildings -nlt PROMOTE_TO_MULTI -lco GEOMETRY_NAME=geom -progress

# one layer of a GeoPackage, into its own schema
ogr2ogr -f PostgreSQL "PG:..." urbis.gpkg Buildings -nln buildings -lco SCHEMA=urbis

# replace an existing table, or append to it
ogr2ogr ... -overwrite          ogr2ogr ... -append

# only a piece: a filter, an area, a few fields
ogr2ogr ... -where "TYPE = 'maison'" -spat 147000 171000 148000 172000 \
            -select ID,TYPE,AREA

# a shapefile without .prj: state its system; or reproject on the way
ogr2ogr ... -a_srs EPSG:31370               ogr2ogr ... -s_srs EPSG:4326 -t_srs EPSG:31370

# the reverse: PostGIS into a GeoPackage, to take away
ogr2ogr -f GPKG ixelles.gpkg "PG:..." \
  -sql "SELECT * FROM municipalities WHERE namefre = 'Ixelles'"

# Oracle into PostGIS directly, if your GDAL has the OCI driver
# (ogrinfo --formats | grep OCI)
ogr2ogr -f PostgreSQL "PG:..." "OCI:user/pass@service:BATIMENTS" -nln buildings
  • -nlt PROMOTE_TO_MULTI: a shapefile often mixes Polygon and MultiPolygon; the PostGIS column accepts only one type.
  • The GIST index is created by default (-lco SPATIAL_INDEX=GIST), and the copy goes through COPY, PostgreSQL's fast path. For millions of rows: -gt 65536, transactions of 65,536 features instead of 20,000.
  • -lco FID=id: the primary key's name (otherwise ogc_fid). It becomes render2d's FID (§50).

Why the index matters: a 1 km² view of the UrbIS buildings
  1. The table: 247,625 buildings over about 162 km² (the Region).
  2. Without an index (Seq Scan): PostgreSQL reads the 247,625 rows and tests each rectangle.
  3. With the index: it walks down the tree and reads only the view's buildings. On average 247,625 ÷ 162 ≈ 1,530 per km², many more in the Pentagon, many fewer in Watermael-Boitsfort.
  4. About 160 times fewer rows read, and as many fewer over the network.

68. A Windows executable, unzipped anywhere

Built under MSYS2, render2d.exe runs fine in the UCRT64 terminal: there, /ucrt64/bin is in the PATH and GDAL knows where its files are. On a PC without MSYS2, two things are missing: the DLLs (GDAL, Cairo, PROJ, GEOS… about a hundred of them), and the reference files that GDAL and PROJ read at run time. The release zip carries both, in one folder.

The DLLs: next to the executable

When Windows starts a program, it looks for each DLL first in the program's own folder, then in the system folders, then in the PATH. So a DLL placed next to render2d.exe is always found, whatever the PATH. Which ones? ldd render2d.exe lists every DLL loaded, those needed by the DLLs included; we keep those coming from /ucrt64/bin. The Windows ones (kernel32.dll, ucrtbase.dll…) are on every PC.

The reference files: share/ next to the executable

  • PROJ reads proj.db, a SQLite database of every coordinate system: "EPSG:31370" is found there, with its ellipsoid and its parameters. Without it, a .prj or a GeoJSON's crs is not recognised.
  • GDAL reads its share/gdal folder: driver descriptions, some tables. Fewer things depend on it, but some drivers need it.

Both look in the folder fixed at build time (/ucrt64/share/…), which does not exist elsewhere. Since 0.4.4, gdalInit() looks beside the executable, before registering the drivers:

Who decides, in order
  1. The executable's folder: GetModuleFileNameW on Windows (the true path, even when started from the PATH), /proc/self/exe on Linux.
  2. GDAL: if GDAL_DATA is set in the environment, it wins (the user knows best). Otherwise, if <exe>\share\gdal exists: CPLSetConfigOption("GDAL_DATA", …).
  3. PROJ: if PROJ_DATA (or the older PROJ_LIB) is set, it wins. Otherwise, if <exe>\share\proj\proj.db exists: OSRSetPROJSearchPaths.
  4. Otherwise nothing changes: under MSYS2, the built-in paths work as before.

render2d --version says what was chosen, so a doubt is settled in one command:

render2d 0.4.4 — step 4a (0.4.4: GDAL and PROJ data beside the executable)
…
GDAL data: C:\Tools\render2d-0.4.4-windows-x64\share\gdal (beside the executable)
PROJ data: C:\Tools\render2d-0.4.4-windows-x64\share\proj (beside the executable)

The big transformation grids of PROJ (*.tif, hundreds of MB) are left out: a Lambert 72 map does not need them, and PROJ can fetch one on demand if a precise datum shift ever does.

The script: tools/package-windows.sh

In the UCRT64 terminal: build the Release, copy the executable and its DLLs, share/gdal and share/proj, the styles, the licenses; then check: start it with a PATH holding only C:\Windows and the environment empty, and draw a small square in EPSG:31370. If a DLL is missing, it does not start; if proj.db is not found, the report does not name EPSG:31370. Only then the zip: dist/render2d-<version>-windows-x64.zip.

The licenses

render2d is AGPL; the libraries shipped with it keep their own licenses (MIT, LGPL, BSD…), and most of them ask that their text travel with the binaries. pacman -Qqo gives the MSYS2 package of each DLL; the script copies its share/licenses/<package> folder and writes the list of packages with their versions in licenses/PACKAGES.txt. The LGPL libraries (Cairo, among others) are DLLs: a user can replace them, which is what that license asks.

Publishing: a GitHub Release

The zip does not go into git (dist/ is ignored): it is attached to a Release, a page tied to a tag (v0.4.4), where anyone downloads it without cloning. Later, a GitHub Action could build it on every tag, on a Windows machine with MSYS2: the same script, run by someone else.

  1. Step 1 — Matrix, canvas, Cairo surface: test shapes in PNG, SVG and PDF. ✓
  2. Step 2 — GDAL/OGR reading: drawing a real layer with a fixed style. ✓
  3. Step 3 — Styles: the reduced SLD model, written in YSLD. 3a: symbolizers and scale ranges ✓; 3b: attributes and filters ✓.
  4. Step 3c — Labels: the text symbolizer (label: ${POLICENUM}), the font, the halo, arithmetic in expressions, rotation from CARTOANGLE, the pixel ratio of high-density screens ✓.
  5. Step 3d — Symbols: the well-known shapes, rotation and size by attribute (proportional symbols, unit of measure), PNG images, hatching anchored to the ground ✓. Later: SVG with librsvg.
  6. Step 3e — The map as a library: r2d_map (open layers, styles read once, shared report), CairoImage (a surface in memory), programs only when render2d is the main project. No rendering change, proven by a characterisation test. First client: editor2d. ✓
  7. Step 3f — Querying the map: the FID, the same rule code to draw and to query (RuleMatcher), the exact point / line / polygon test, Map::queryAt and --at. First client: editor2d's selection. ✓
  8. Step 3g — Querying an area: orient and segment crossing, the three cases (vertex inside, crossing edges, area inside the feature), the cap, Map::queryArea, --box and --area. Client: editor2d's rectangle and polygon tools. ✓
  9. Step 3h ✓ — --config viewer.yaml (§57): the configuration reader moves from editor2d into render2d, shared by both; paths relative to the file; the command line has the last word; the report says what was read; visible and title already accepted for editor2d's project.
  10. Step 3i ✓ — A map that changes (§58): addLayer, removeLayer, moveLayer, Map::open through addLayer, the default colour following the layer. Client: editor2d's step 3a.
  11. Step 3j ✓ — Writing YSLD (§59): writeYsld, the round-trip test on every style, Map::setLayerStyle. Client: editor2d's legend dialog (3b-1).
  12. Step 3k ✓ — The property catalogue (§60): what each symbolizer can receive, the add and remove functions, the starting value. Client: the "+" of editor2d's legend dialog (3b-2).
  13. Step 3l ✓ — Writing the map (§61): writeConfig, relative paths, viewer2d's keys kept. Client: editor2d's project (3c).
  14. Step 3m ✓ — Classifying (§62): a field's values, the breaks (equal intervals, quantiles), the Categorize and Graduate rules. Client: editor2d's assistants (3b-3).
  15. Step 3n ✓ — Classifying on the command line (§63): --categorize, --graduate, --write-style; classify(), the same function for editor2d and render2d.
  16. Step 3o ✓ — Selecting by attributes (§64): Map::queryWhere, GdalLayer::forFids, --where. Client: editor2d's selection by expression (4a).
  17. Step 4a ✓ — Rasters (§65, §66): reading the view's window with its overviews, bands into ARGB32, transparent nodata, the raster symbolizer and its opacity. Same coordinate system as the map. Client: editor2d's raster layers.
  18. Step 4b — On-the-fly reprojection (PROJ), for vectors and rasters.
  19. Later, after reprojection and PostGIS, once editor2d and viewer2d are rich enough: render2d as a server, a program that stays up with its map open and answers over HTTP (/map, /tiles/z/x/y, /query, /legend, a style changed live) and as a standard WMS, readable by QGIS and any GIS client. The favoured option: render2d --serve, a process that stays up with its map open and talks through a pipe (stdin/stdout), one JSON line per request and per answer, the image as bytes right after, with the vocabulary of editor2d's commands (map.render, layer.add…). In front of it, FastAPI (or viewer2d) keeps a small pool of these processes: a GDAL crash kills only one, they draw in parallel. The other paths stay open: an HTTP server in C++ inside render2d, or render2d as a Python module (pybind11) if every millisecond counts. Then, real time: telling the server an area has changed, redrawing, invalidating the tiles.
  20. For the map built in editor2d (its notes, §31 to §34): describing the format itself (properties, types, default values, geometries concerned) for the legend dialog and the future API; reading a field's distinct values, minimum and maximum.
  21. Alongside, in a separate project: the test viewer viewer2d (NiceGUI + Leaflet, in Lambert 72), which calls render2d on the command line at every map move. ✓
  22. Step 4 — Reprojection, clipping to the extent, simplification in pixels. First try: a source in WGS 84 (EPSG:4326) on the Lambert 72 map, first with render2d as it is (it draws it without reprojecting, with a warning), to see the problem before solving it.
  23. Step 5 — The complete command line, with warnings in JSON. Idea noted: without --style, look for a style of the same name next to the data (UrbISBuildings_04000_Addresses.yaml next to the .shp), as QGIS does with its .qml. Beware: tiles2d and viewer2d will then have to include that file in the cache key. Order matters: first resolve the style (which file will be used), then compute the key with that file, then pass it explicitly to render2d with --style, so that the style drawn is necessarily the key's.
  24. Step 6 — z/x/y tiles for Leaflet. They do not go into render2d: a separate tool, tiles2d, cuts the pyramid and calls render2d for each tile, like gdal2tiles.py on top of GDAL (see chapter 10 of viewer2d's notes).
  25. Step 7 — Vector tiles (MVT): the same reading, clipping and simplification steps, an MVT encoder next to the canvas, and the translation of YSLD into a MapLibre style.

The "style" and "reprojection" steps were swapped after the first rendering of Brussels: all the test data are in Lambert 72, and the most visible need was style (outlines wider than the buildings at small scale).

Versions follow this roadmap: 0.<step>.<sub-step>, so 0.3.4 for step 3d, 0.3.5 for step 3e, 0.3.6 for step 3f, 0.3.7 for step 3g, 0.3.8 for step 3h, 0.3.9 for step 3i, 0.3.10 for step 3j, 0.3.11 for step 3k, 0.3.12 for step 3l, 0.3.13 for step 3m, 0.3.14 for step 3n (0.3.15: scale bands; 0.3.16: reclassifying), 0.3.17 for step 3o, 0.4.0 for step 4a (0.4.1: listLayers; 0.4.2: the session password; 0.4.3: tables without geometry; for editor2d; 0.4.4: the data beside the executable, for the Windows zip, §68). render2d --version shows it, with the build date and the GDAL and Cairo versions, and the report starts with it: a forgotten executable is spotted immediately.

Desirable, without a date: label collisions and labels straddling tile edges.

Then: profile and autostyle, then a custom engine (AGG or Blend2D) behind the same canvas.
